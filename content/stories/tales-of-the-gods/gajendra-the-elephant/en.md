---
title: Gajendra, the Elephant Who Called Out
summary: The strongest elephant in the forest learns that even the strongest sometimes need to call for help.
moral: It is brave to ask for help.
reviewStatus: approved
reviewer: Satwik Rudra
reviewedAt: 2026-09-03

In a forest at the foot of a great mountain there lived a herd of elephants, and their king was called Gajendra.

Gajendra was enormous. His legs were like tree trunks. His ears were like sails. When he walked, the ground hummed, and when he trumpeted, birds a mile away looked up. He had never once in his life needed help from anybody, and he was rather proud of that.

One hot afternoon Gajendra led his herd to a lake in the middle of the forest to bathe. It was a lovely lake, deep and green and covered in lotus flowers, with cool mud along the edge that was perfect for rolling in.

The baby elephants splashed. The mothers sprayed water over their backs. And Gajendra waded out into the very deepest part, where the water came up to his chin and the lotuses bumped against his trunk, and he closed his eyes and sighed with happiness.

That was when something took hold of his leg.

Deep down in the green water there lived an old crocodile, older than anyone knew, and grumpier than anyone could imagine. He had been asleep in the mud. Gajendra's great foot had come down right next to his nose. And the crocodile, without even thinking about it, had opened his jaws and clamped them around the elephant's ankle.

It did not hurt. The crocodile's grip was more like being held by a very strong, very stubborn hand. But it held.

Gajendra pulled. The crocodile pulled back.

Gajendra pulled harder. The crocodile dug his feet into the mud and did not let go.

"Let go of me!" trumpeted Gajendra. "I am the king of the elephants!"

The crocodile, who did not care about kings, said nothing at all and held on.

The herd gathered at the edge of the lake. The mothers wrapped their trunks around Gajendra's trunk and pulled. The young elephants pushed from behind. But the mud was deep and the crocodile was stubborn, and the sun went down, and still Gajendra stood in the lake with one leg held fast.

He pulled all night. He pulled all the next day. Slowly, slowly, the great king grew tired. His legs trembled. His ears drooped. For the very first time in his life, Gajendra understood that he could not do this by himself.

And that frightened him more than the crocodile did.

So he did something he had never done before. He lifted his trunk out of the water, and he reached up, high, toward the sky, with a single lotus flower held in the tip of it.

"Help me," he said. Not a trumpet. Not a roar. Just a small, honest voice. "Please. I cannot do this alone."

Far away, in a palace made of light, the great god Vishnu heard him.

Vishnu was very busy, as gods are. But he heard the small voice, and he saw the tired elephant holding up his one flower, and he did not wait one moment. He came down from the sky on the back of a great golden eagle, so fast that the wind of it flattened the lotus leaves.

He landed at the edge of the lake. He waded into the water, quite unafraid, and he put one gentle hand on the crocodile's rough head.

"Old friend," he said softly, "it is time to let go."

And the crocodile, who had held on for two days and two nights out of pure stubbornness, felt the hand on his head and felt, for the first time in years, that somebody was speaking to him kindly. He opened his jaws. He let go. And he sank slowly back down into the green mud to think about that.

Gajendra lifted his foot. He was free.

He stood in the lake, dripping and trembling, and he looked at Vishnu, who was smiling at him.

"I only had to ask," said the elephant.

"You only had to ask," agreed Vishnu. "That is often the hardest part. But you did it." Then he took the lotus flower from Gajendra's trunk, tucked it behind his ear, climbed onto his eagle, and was gone into the evening sky.

The herd came thundering into the water and surrounded their king, touching him with their trunks to make sure he was real. The babies climbed on his back. The mothers rumbled with relief.

That night Gajendra lay down under a banyan tree with his whole herd around him. He was still enormous. He was still the king. But he was a little softer now, in the way that only asking for help can make you.

The lake grew still. The lotuses closed. And far down in the green mud, an old crocodile fell asleep, dreaming of a kind hand on his head.
