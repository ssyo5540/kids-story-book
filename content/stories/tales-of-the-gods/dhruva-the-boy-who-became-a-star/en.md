If you look up on a clear night and find the one star that never moves, the star that all the other stars circle around, you are looking at Dhruva.

But before he was a star, Dhruva was a little boy. This is how it happened.

Long ago there was a king named Uttanapada who had two wives and two small sons. The elder queen was called Suniti, and her son was Dhruva. The younger queen was called Suruchi, and her son was Uttama.

The king loved both his boys. But Suruchi was a mixed-up sort of queen. She had got it into her head that there was only enough love in the palace for one child, and she wanted that child to be hers.

One afternoon, when Dhruva was five years old, he wandered into the great hall and saw his father sitting on the throne with Uttama on his lap. The king was tickling him, and Uttama was laughing.

Dhruva wanted to be tickled too. So he climbed the steps of the throne and reached up to his father.

Before the king could lift him, Suruchi stepped in between.

"No," she said. "Not you. That lap is for my son. If you want to sit on the king's lap, little Dhruva, you will have to be born again as my child. Go back to your mother."

The hall went quiet. The king opened his mouth and then closed it again, because he was a kind man but not a very brave one, and he did not know what to say.

Dhruva looked at his father. He looked at his brother, who had stopped laughing. He looked at Suruchi. Then he turned around, walked down the steps, and went to find his mother.

He did not cry until he found her. Then he cried a great deal.

Suniti held him and rocked him and wiped his face with the end of her sari. She did not say a single unkind word about Suruchi. She only said, "Some people have very small hearts, my love. They cannot help it. It does not mean there is anything wrong with you."

"But I wanted to sit with Papa," said Dhruva.

"I know."

"Where can I sit where nobody can send me away?"

Suniti thought for a long moment. She was a gentle queen, and she believed in telling children the truth.

"There is only one lap like that," she said softly. "It belongs to Vishnu, who looks after the whole world. Nobody can push you off it, because it is not a lap anyone can own. But he is very hard to find. Great sages spend their whole lives looking for him."

Dhruva stopped crying. He sat up straight.

"Then I will find him," he said.

He said it the way five-year-olds say things, as though it were the simplest matter in the world. And before his mother could answer, he had kissed her cheek, climbed down from her lap, and walked out of the palace, past the gardens, past the fields, and into the great green forest beyond.

Suniti watched him go with her hand over her heart. She wanted to run after him. But something told her that this little boy was walking toward something important, and that the kindest thing she could do was to let him walk.

The forest was very big and Dhruva was very small. He walked until his legs ached. He walked until the sun went down and the trees turned to shadows. He was not exactly afraid, but he was not exactly not afraid either, and he held his own hand for company.

At last he came to a clearing, and in the clearing sat an old man with a long white beard and a small stringed instrument on his knee. It was Narada, the wandering sage, who travelled between heaven and earth and knew nearly everything.

"Little one," said Narada, "where are you going?"

"To find Vishnu," said Dhruva.

Narada stroked his beard. "Do you know how hard that is? Wise men sit in caves for a hundred years. They eat only leaves. They do not sleep. And even then, most of them never see him."

"I do not mind," said Dhruva.

"Would you not rather go home? Your mother must be worried. Your father will change his mind. Boys your age should be playing, not sitting in forests."

Dhruva shook his head. "I have been sent away once," he said. "I am not going to be sent away from this too. Please. Tell me what to do."

Narada looked at the small determined face for a long time. Then he laughed, not unkindly, but the way you laugh when you meet someone much braver than you expected.

"Very well," he said. "Sit under that tree by the river. Close your eyes. And say these words, over and over, with your whole heart." And he leaned down and whispered a few words in Dhruva's ear.

Dhruva sat under the tree. He closed his eyes. He began to say the words.

He said them all night, while the owls called.

He said them all the next day, while the sun crossed the sky, and the next night, and the next.

The forest animals came to look at him. A deer lay down beside him. A parrot perched on his shoulder. Ants walked over his toes, and he did not move. When it rained, he sat in the rain. When the wind blew, he sat in the wind. When he grew hungry he ate a few fallen fruits, and when he grew sleepy he slept sitting up, and when he woke he began the words again.

The seasons turned. The leaves fell and grew back. And little by little, the words stopped being something Dhruva said and became something Dhruva was. He grew very still. He grew very quiet. He glowed, just a little, the way a coal does under the ash.

Then one morning, the light in the clearing changed.

Dhruva opened his eyes. Standing before him, with a smile as wide as the sky, was Vishnu himself, blue as deep water, with a lotus in one hand and the whole world's kindness in his face.

Dhruva tried to say something. But he had been saying the same few words for so long that no others would come. So he simply looked, and Vishnu looked back, and that was enough.

"Little Dhruva," said Vishnu at last. "You have sat here longer than men ten times your age. What is it you want?"

Dhruva thought about the throne, and the lap, and the queen who had pushed him away. And he found, to his surprise, that he did not want any of that any more.

"I wanted a place that nobody could take away from me," he said. "But I think I have already found it. I am sitting in it."

Vishnu laughed, and the river laughed with him.

"You have," he said. "And because you found it so young and held it so steadily, I will give you something more. When your long life is over, you will not simply rest. You will become a star. Not just any star. The star that does not move. The one that every other star circles around, and that travellers steer by, and that mothers point to when they tell their children stories at night."

"A star," whispered Dhruva.

"The steadiest one," said Vishnu. "Because you were the steadiest boy. Now go home. Your father misses you, and your mother has been counting the days."

So Dhruva walked back through the forest. It seemed much smaller than it had before.

At the palace gates his father was waiting. The king ran to him, and lifted him up, and set him on his own shoulders, which is higher than any lap, and carried him inside. Suniti wept and laughed at once. Even Suruchi came, with her head bowed and her eyes red, and said she was sorry, and meant it. Dhruva forgave her, because a boy who has sat with Vishnu has very little room left for grudges.

He grew up to be a good king, patient and quiet and steady, and he ruled for a long, long time.

And when at last his life was done, the sky opened, and Dhruva rose up, and up, and up, until he was a single point of silver light, right at the top of the world.

He is still there tonight.

If you go to the window, you can find him. All the other stars turn slowly around him through the night, like children around a father's chair. But Dhruva stays exactly where he is, steady and bright, in the place that nobody can ever take away.

Close your eyes. He is keeping watch.
