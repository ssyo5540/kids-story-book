In a village called Gokul, on the banks of a wide slow river, there were more cows than people. There were brown cows and white cows and cows with soft grey spots, and every morning the whole village smelled of warm milk.

In the middle of this village lived a little boy named Krishna. He had dark curly hair, a peacock feather tucked behind one ear, and a smile that made even the grumpiest grandmother forget what she had been grumbling about.

Krishna's mother was called Yashoda. She loved him more than the moon loves the night sky, which is a very great deal. And she spent a great deal of her time looking for him, because Krishna was never where he had been a moment ago.

Now, of all the good things in Gokul, the very best was butter.

Every morning Yashoda sat by her churn with a long wooden paddle and churned the cream from the evening's milk. Round and round went the paddle. Splash and thump went the cream. And slowly, slowly, little golden lumps of butter rose to the top, soft and sweet and cool.

Krishna would sit beside her and watch with wide eyes.

"Just a little?" he would ask.

"After your breakfast," Yashoda would say.

"Just a very little?"

"After your breakfast, Krishna."

And she would scoop the butter into a clay pot and hang the pot from the ceiling on a rope, high up where small hands could not reach, the way every mother in Gokul did.

But Krishna was not every child.

One morning, when Yashoda had gone to the river to fetch water, Krishna stood in the middle of the room and looked up at the butter pot swinging gently from its rope. He looked at it for a long time. Then he went to the door and whistled.

His friends came running. There was Sudama, who was tall for his age, and Madhumangal, who was round, and half a dozen other little cowherd boys with dusty feet and hopeful faces.

"Butter," said Krishna, pointing up.

The boys looked at the pot. They looked at each other. And then, without a word, because they had done this before, they began to climb.

Madhumangal knelt on the floor, being the roundest and the steadiest. Sudama stood on his back. A smaller boy climbed onto Sudama's shoulders. And Krishna, being the lightest and the quickest, scrambled up all three of them like a monkey up a mango tree, and reached the pot, and tipped it, and out came the butter, in soft golden lumps, straight into his open hands.

He ate some. He passed some down to Sudama, who passed some to Madhumangal, who was very patient at the bottom. And when the boys were full, Krishna did something that not all butter thieves would do.

He carried the rest outside, where a family of monkeys sat waiting in the neem tree, and he shared it with them. Then he went to the cowshed and gave the last of it to a thin little calf whose mother was away in the pasture.

"There," he said, wiping his hands on his shirt. "Now everyone has had some."

By the time Yashoda came home with her water jar, the room was empty, the pot was swinging gently from its rope, and there was not so much as a smear of butter left in it.

"Krishna?" she called.

No answer. Only, from somewhere behind the house, the sound of a small boy laughing.

This happened the next day. And the next. Yashoda hung the pot higher. The boys built a taller tower. Yashoda hid the pot behind the grain jars. Krishna found it before she had finished hiding it.

She tied a little brass bell to the rope, so that it would ring if anyone touched the pot. The next morning the bell did not ring, and the butter was gone, and inside the bell, packed in tight, was a tiny ball of cotton.

She asked her neighbour's grandmother to sit in the room and keep watch. The grandmother sat, and watched, and Krishna came and climbed into her lap and told her a story about a cow who could fly, and by the end of the story the grandmother was fast asleep with a smile on her face, and the butter, once again, was gone.

And it was not only Yashoda's butter. Soon the whole village knew that little Krishna had a nose for butter the way a bee has a nose for flowers.

One afternoon a line of neighbours came to Yashoda's door.

"Your Krishna broke my pot," said one. "Not on purpose, but it broke."

"Your Krishna ate my whole morning's butter," said another, "and then he kissed my baby on the head and ran off, so I could not even stay cross."

"Your Krishna," said a third, and then she stopped, and sighed, and shook her head, and smiled, because it is very hard to complain about a boy with a peacock feather in his hair.

Yashoda promised to speak to him. She thanked the neighbours. She closed the door. And she went looking.

She found him in the back room. He was sitting on the floor with his back to her, very still, very quiet, and very busy.

"Krishna," said Yashoda.

He turned around.

His cheeks were round with butter. His chin was shiny with butter. There was butter on his nose, butter in his curls, and a lump of butter sitting in the middle of his palm like a little golden bird.

"I did not eat any butter," said Krishna.

Yashoda put her hands on her hips. She tried to frown. It was very difficult.

"Krishna," she said. "It is on your face."

Krishna reached up and felt his face. He looked at his buttery fingers with great surprise.

"Perhaps," he said thoughtfully, "the monkeys put it there while I was asleep."

That was too much. Yashoda scooped him up, butter and all, and carried him out to the yard, and set him down beside the big stone mortar where the grain was ground.

"You will sit here," she said, "right beside this mortar, where I can see you from the kitchen window, until I have finished my work. And you will not move."

"Yes, Amma," said Krishna.

He sat. Yashoda went inside. She watched him from the window, and for a while he really did sit there, drawing circles in the dust with one finger.

Then a small sound came from the cowshed. It was the thin little calf, calling for its mother, who was still out in the pasture.

Krishna looked at the calf. He looked at the kitchen window. He looked at the calf again.

Then he got up, very quietly, took the last lump of butter that was still hidden in his fist, walked over to the cowshed, and held it out on his flat little palm. The calf licked it up with its rough pink tongue, and sighed, and leaned its head against his shoulder.

Yashoda saw the whole thing from the window.

She meant to march out and scold him. She really did. But her feet would not move, and her eyes filled up, and all the crossness drained out of her like water out of a cracked pot.

She went out into the yard, knelt down beside her son and the calf, and put her arms around both of them.

"You are the most trouble in the world," she said into his buttery hair.

"I know," said Krishna comfortably.

"Tomorrow," said Yashoda, "you will ask before you take."

"I will ask," said Krishna. And he did, too, most of the time, for at least a week.

That evening the whole village gathered under the big banyan tree, the way they did when the work was done. Somebody had brought a drum. Somebody else had brought a flute. The neighbours who had complained that afternoon were laughing now, telling the story of the butter on Krishna's nose, and the story grew a little every time someone told it.

The cows came home from the pasture in a slow brown river, bells clinking, and the thin little calf ran to meet its mother at last and pushed its head against her side. The grandmothers passed around a bowl of warm milk with jaggery in it. And Krishna, who should by rights have been in trouble, sat in the middle of everything with a lump of jaggery in each cheek, listening to the drum.

Krishna fell asleep in his mother's lap before the last song was over. His curls were still a bit sticky. His peacock feather had slipped over one eye.

Yashoda carried him home through the dark, under the stars, past the sleeping cows, and laid him in his bed. She hung tomorrow's butter pot from its rope, just a little higher than before.

Then she sat beside him for a long time, watching him breathe.

And in the neem tree outside, the monkeys settled down for the night, and the little calf slept with its mother, and the whole village of Gokul smelled, very faintly, of butter.
