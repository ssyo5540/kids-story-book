## The Gods Lose Their Shine

At the top of the world, above the clouds, there was once a city of gods. It had golden roofs and gardens that never stopped flowering, and the gods who lived there shone, quite literally, like lamps.

Indra was their king. He rode a white elephant and carried the thunder, and he was, if we are honest, a little proud of himself.

One morning a wandering sage came to visit, a wild-haired old man named Durvasa who was famous for two things: his wisdom, and his temper. He brought Indra a gift, a garland of flowers that never faded, which he had been given by a goddess and carried a very long way.

Indra took the garland with a nod. He did not really look at it. He was thinking about something else, the way kings do. And after a moment he hung the garland on the trunk of his white elephant, who was standing beside him.

The elephant, who was only an elephant, sniffed the flowers, found that they tickled, and shook them off onto the ground.

Durvasa's face went very red.

"That garland," he said, "was a gift. And you have thrown it in the dust. Very well, King Indra. Since you value nothing, let us see how you like having nothing."

He turned and walked away, and behind him a strange thing began to happen.

The gods began to fade.

It was slow at first. Indra noticed that his thunder was quieter than usual. The gardens looked a little grey. The golden roofs seemed to need polishing. Day by day the shine went out of everything, and out of everyone, until the gods stood in their dull city looking at their own dull hands and feeling tired all the way through.

"What is happening to us?" they asked.

Nobody knew. But everybody knew who might.

So they went, all of them together, down through the clouds and across the world to the great ocean of milk, where Vishnu, the keeper of all things, lay resting on the coils of a thousand-headed serpent, dreaming the world along.

Vishnu opened his eyes and looked at the faded gods, and he did not laugh, though he might have.

"You have lost your brightness," he said. "That is not a small thing. But it can be found again."

"How?" asked Indra, who had learned some humility on the long walk down.

"At the bottom of this ocean," said Vishnu, "there is a nectar called amrita. Whoever drinks it becomes bright and strong and never fades. If you want it, you must churn the ocean until the nectar rises to the top, the way butter rises out of milk."

Indra looked at the ocean of milk. It stretched to the horizon in every direction, white and still under a white sky.

"Churn it?" he said. "All of it? We could not do that in a thousand years."

"No," agreed Vishnu. "Not alone."

He let that sit for a moment.

"You will need your cousins," he said.

The gods groaned. Their cousins were the asuras, who lived on the other side of the sky. The asuras were not wicked, whatever the gods liked to say about them. But they were loud, and strong, and stubborn, and the two families had been quarrelling since before anyone could remember.

"They will never help us," said Indra.

"Have you asked?" said Vishnu.

So Indra went to the asuras, which was the hardest thing he had done in a long time, and he asked. And the asura king, who was called Bali and who was not nearly as unreasonable as the gods believed, folded his big arms and said, "If there is nectar, we want half."

"Half," agreed Indra, and they shook hands, and for the first time in a thousand years the two families stood together on the shore of the ocean of milk, ready to work.

That night the gods slept on the sand. They were still faded and still tired. But something had already changed, though none of them could have said what it was.

## The Mountain and the Serpent

You cannot churn an ocean with a wooden paddle. You need something much, much bigger.

So the gods and the asuras went together to a mountain called Mandara, which was so tall that clouds got stuck on it, and they dug around its roots and lifted it, all of them together, straining and heaving, and carried it down to the shore. That was the churning stick.

Then they needed a rope.

The rope was Vasuki, the king of the serpents, who was long enough to wrap around a mountain three times with plenty left over. He agreed to help, though he asked them politely not to pull too hard.

The gods took hold of his tail. The asuras, who wanted the grander end, took hold of his head. They wound him around the mountain. And they began to pull, first one side and then the other, so that the mountain turned in the milk with a great slow rumbling, like the beginning of a storm.

For about a minute, it worked beautifully.

Then the mountain began to sink.

It was so heavy, and the ocean was so deep, that Mandara simply slid down through the milk, and the gods and asuras found themselves pulling on a serpent wrapped around nothing at all.

"Stop! Stop!" they cried, and they let go, and everyone sat down on the sand, hot and disappointed.

But Vishnu had been watching, and Vishnu had a way of being exactly what was needed.

He slipped into the ocean of milk without a splash, and under the surface he changed. His arms became flippers. His back became a great round shell. He became a tortoise, the largest tortoise there has ever been, as wide as an island and as patient as a stone, and he swam down beneath the sinking mountain and lifted it, gently, onto his back.

"Now," said his voice from far below, "pull."

The gods took the tail. The asuras took the head. And the mountain turned again, steady this time, resting on the back of the great tortoise, while the milk began to swirl.

Round and round. Pull and give. Pull and give.

It was hard work. The sun crossed the sky and set. The stars came out and went in. The gods pulled, and the asuras pulled, and slowly they found a rhythm together, the way rowers do, until nobody could tell which side was which. Somebody began to sing, low and steady, to keep the time. Somebody on the other side joined in.

Vasuki the serpent, being pulled back and forth for hours, grew rather warm, and now and then a puff of hot breath came out of his mouth over the asuras, who wiped their faces and did not complain.

The milk went from white to cream. From cream to gold. It began to froth and foam, and small waves ran up the beach and back.

"Something is coming," said Indra, wiping his forehead. "I can feel it."

Far below, on the back of the great tortoise, the mountain turned and turned. The ocean sighed. The gods and asuras pulled together under the stars, tired to their bones and, for the first time in a thousand years, on the same side.

And the milk began to give up its secrets.

## Wonders Rise

The first thing that rose out of the churning ocean was a cow.

She came up slowly, white as the milk itself, with soft dark eyes and a bell around her neck that rang by itself. Her name was Kamadhenu, and she was the mother of all cows, and whatever anyone wished for she could give, if the wish was kind.

Everyone stopped pulling to look. Then they started again, faster.

Next came a horse, pure white with seven heads, who stepped out of the foam and shook his manes and galloped off along the beach with his hooves ringing. The asuras cheered.

Then an elephant, white as well, with four great tusks. Indra's own elephant, the one who had shaken off the garland, looked at this newcomer and lowered his head a little, as if to say, "Well, that is fair."

Then a jewel, the size of a fist, that glowed with every colour and none. Then a tree covered in flowers that never fell, whose scent could be smelled from the far side of the world. Then a whole crowd of dancing girls in silver and blue, laughing, who spun away across the water like leaves on a wind.

Every time something new rose up, the gods and the asuras both shouted, and both said, "That one is ours!" and both, after some grumbling, agreed to share.

Then the churning brought up the moon.

He rose out of the milk quite small, a round pale disc, and he did not seem to know where he was. He drifted. Nobody knew quite what to do with a moon. At last Shiva, the quiet god who sat a little apart from the others, reached out and lifted the moon and set him in his own tangled hair, where he has stayed ever since, and where he was very comfortable.

And then, in a shower of golden light, standing on a lotus in the middle of the ocean, came Lakshmi.

She was the goddess of good fortune, and she was more beautiful than anything they had ever seen. Everybody, gods and asuras alike, went completely silent. Lakshmi looked at them all with a small smile. Then she looked down through the milk to where the great tortoise held up the mountain, and she smiled much more, and she went to Vishnu and stayed with him from that day on.

Everyone was so happy that they did not notice, at first, that the milk had turned dark.

It came up from the very bottom, a black cloud, spreading through the ocean like ink in water. It was the poison called Halahala, everything bitter and harmful that had ever sunk to the bottom of the world, all stirred up together. Where it touched the sand, the sand went grey. Where its smell reached the shore, the gods and asuras coughed and staggered and dropped the serpent.

"Stop!" they cried. "We must stop! It will spread everywhere!"

But you cannot stop a poison by stopping. Somebody had to take it away.

Shiva stood up.

He walked down the beach to the edge of the dark water, and he knelt, and he cupped his hands, and he gathered up the poison, every drop, until he held all the bitterness of the world in his two hands. Then he drank it.

His wife, Parvati, ran to him and pressed her hand against his throat, so that the poison would go no further than that.

And there it stayed. Shiva's throat turned as blue as the evening sky, a deep and beautiful blue, and it has been blue ever since, so that people call him Neelakantha, the blue-throated one. It did not hurt him. He was Shiva. But he sat down afterward for a long time with the moon in his hair and his eyes closed, and nobody disturbed him.

The ocean, freed of its poison, turned gold and clear again.

"Thank you," said Indra, and all the gods and asuras said it after him, and Shiva opened one eye and nodded.

They took hold of the serpent. They began, once more, to pull.

## The Nectar

The last thing to rise out of the ocean of milk was a young man carrying a pot.

He was called Dhanvantari, and he would one day teach people about herbs and healing. But nobody was looking at him. Everybody was looking at the pot.

It was made of gold, and it glowed from the inside, and it was full to the brim with a liquid like liquid moonlight. Amrita. The nectar. The thing they had all been churning for.

For one heartbeat everybody just stared.

Then the asuras, who were standing nearer, and who were, after all, very quick, snatched it.

"Half!" cried Indra. "We agreed on half!"

"We will talk about half," said the asuras, holding the pot high, "after we have had a sip."

And that would have been that, and the story might have gone very badly, if Vishnu had not been paying attention.

He came up out of the ocean, not as a tortoise this time, but as a dancer.

She was called Mohini, and she was the most enchanting dancer who ever lived. Her anklets chimed. Her silks were the colour of sunset. She danced along the shore toward the asuras with the golden pot, and they forgot, every one of them, what they had been about to do.

"Such a beautiful pot," said Mohini. "Such a lot of quarrelling over it. Let me pour. I will give everyone a share, and nobody will have to fight."

The asuras looked at each other. This seemed perfectly reasonable. They handed her the pot.

Mohini smiled, and she began to pour, and she poured for the gods first.

One by one they drank. And one by one, as the nectar touched their lips, the shine came back into them. Indra's thunder rumbled in his chest. The grey went out of his hands. Around him, the gods lit up like lamps at dusk, one after another, until the whole beach glowed.

The asuras, watching, began to realise they had been tricked.

One of them, a clever one named Rahu, slipped in among the gods in disguise and managed to swallow a single sip before the sun and the moon spotted him and cried out. Vishnu turned, and Rahu fled, but that one sip was already inside him and could not be taken back. So Rahu became a shadow, a dark shape that lives in the sky, and every now and then, out of old habit, he tries to swallow the moon, and the moon goes dark for a little while, and then slips out of his mouth again and shines. That is what an eclipse is. It never lasts.

The rest of the asuras did not get their half. They were angry about it for quite a long time. But the gods, who had learned something on the beach, did not gloat. Some of them, in later years, quietly found ways to make it up.

And when the pot was empty and the shore was quiet, the gods sat down in the sand, bright again, and looked at the ocean.

The mountain was back where it belonged. The serpent was uncoiled and asleep. The great tortoise had swum away. The moon sat in Shiva's hair, and Lakshmi sat beside Vishnu, and the white cow grazed along the beach with her bell ringing softly by itself.

"We could not have done it alone," said Indra.

Nobody argued.

The ocean of milk lay still and gold under the stars, giving up no more secrets, and the gods lay down on the warm sand, shining gently, and one by one they closed their eyes.

And the whole world, which had been holding its breath, let it out, and slept.
