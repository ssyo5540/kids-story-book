---
title: The Lion and the Clever Rabbit
summary: A small rabbit with a big idea teaches a greedy lion that the forest is for sharing.
moral: A calm, clever mind is stronger than a loud roar.
reviewStatus: needs_review
---

In a forest full of tall grass and tumbling streams there lived a lion called Bhasuraka. He was large and golden and very proud, and he had one bad habit. He was greedy.

Every day Bhasuraka roared and chased and frightened the animals, far more than any lion needs to. The deer could not graze in peace. The monkeys could not nap in the trees. Even the birds sang more quietly than they used to.

At last the animals gathered under the old banyan tree to talk.

"We cannot go on like this," said the deer.

"Let us make a bargain with him," said the wise old tortoise. "Every day, one of us will go and visit the lion and keep him company. If he has a visitor, perhaps he will stop chasing everyone else."

So they went to Bhasuraka, and the lion, who liked being important, agreed. "Send me one visitor every day," he said, "and I will leave the rest of you alone. But the visitor must never be late."

Day after day the animals took turns. And day after day the lion grew lazier and prouder and grumpier.

Then it was the turn of a small brown rabbit. She had long ears, quick feet, and a mind that was quicker still.

The rabbit did not hurry. She stopped to nibble a clover flower. She stopped to watch the clouds. And she arrived at the lion's cave when the sun was already sinking.

Bhasuraka was pacing up and down. "You are late," he roared. "Why are you so late?"

The rabbit bowed very low. "Forgive me, great king," she said. "I set out early, truly I did. But on the way I met another lion. He said this forest belongs to him, and that he is the real king here."

Bhasuraka's mane bristled. "Another lion? In my forest? Where is he?"

"He lives at the bottom of the old stone well," said the rabbit. "I will show you, if you promise not to be too loud. He is very fierce."

So the lion followed the little rabbit through the trees, grumbling all the way, until they came to the deep stone well at the edge of the meadow.

"He is down there," whispered the rabbit. "Look."

Bhasuraka leaned over the edge and looked down. Far below, in the still dark water, a large golden lion looked back at him, with a bristling mane and angry eyes.

"How dare you!" roared Bhasuraka. And the lion in the well roared back, exactly as loudly. Bhasuraka roared again, louder. So did the other lion.

That was too much. Bhasuraka gathered himself and leaped straight down into the well, to teach the stranger a lesson.

Splash!

There was no other lion, of course. There was only cold, cold water, and a very wet Bhasuraka, paddling in circles and spluttering.

The rabbit did not laugh. She ran and fetched the elephants, and the elephants let down a long, strong vine, and slowly they pulled the dripping lion out of the well and set him on the grass.

Bhasuraka shook the water out of his mane. He looked at the well. He looked at the rabbit. And for the first time in a long time, he felt a little bit silly.

"There was no lion," he said quietly.

"No, great king," said the rabbit. "Only you. There has only ever been one lion in this forest. And a forest is a big enough place for everyone in it."

Bhasuraka thought about this for a long while. Then he lay down in the warm evening grass to dry.

From that day on, the lion was still large and golden, but he was no longer greedy. He roared only at sunrise, to say good morning. And the deer grazed, and the monkeys napped, and the birds sang as loudly as they liked.

As for the rabbit, she went home to her burrow, nibbled one more clover flower, and fell asleep with her long ears folded softly over her eyes.
