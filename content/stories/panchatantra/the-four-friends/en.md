In a forest where the trees grew close together and the light came down in green patches, there was a lake. It was not a large lake. But it had cool water and soft banks and a great many reeds, and it was home to four very different friends.

The first was a crow named Laghupatanaka, who could fly high above the trees and see everything for miles.

The second was a mouse named Hiranyaka, who was small and quick and had teeth that could chew through almost anything.

The third was a tortoise named Mantharaka, who was slow and wise and never, ever hurried.

And the fourth was a deer named Chitranga, who had long thin legs and could run faster than the wind through the tall grass.

They had not always been friends. The crow had once thought mice were only for eating. The mouse had once thought crows were only for hiding from. But one day, long before this story, they had helped each other out of a tangle, and they had discovered that they liked talking more than they liked being afraid. The tortoise had joined them, and then the deer, and now the four of them met every evening on the bank of the lake to share the news of the day.

"The mango tree by the river is in fruit," the crow would say, because he could see it from the sky.

"There is a new hole in the farmer's fence," the mouse would say, because he had made it.

"The water is warmer this week," the tortoise would say, because he felt everything slowly and carefully.

And the deer would tell them about the far meadows where she went to run, and they would all sit together until the stars came out.

One evening, the deer did not come.

The crow, the mouse and the tortoise waited. The sun went down. The frogs began to sing. Still no deer.

"Something is wrong," said the tortoise. "Chitranga is never late."

"I will look," said the crow, and he flew up above the trees, turning his head this way and that.

He searched the meadows. He searched the river bank. And at last, near the edge of the forest, in a place where the hunters sometimes set their traps, he saw her. Chitranga was lying on the ground, tangled in a net of strong brown rope, and she could not get up.

The crow flew down beside her. "Do not be frightened," he said. "We will get you out."

"The hunter will come back in the morning," said the deer, and her voice shook. "He always checks his nets in the morning."

"Then we have all night," said the crow. He flew back to the lake as fast as his wings would carry him.

"Hiranyaka!" he called to the mouse. "You are needed. Climb onto my back."

The mouse had never flown before. He held on tight to the crow's feathers and squeezed his eyes shut, and the crow carried him up over the dark trees and set him down beside the deer.

Hiranyaka looked at the net. It was made of thick rope, knotted again and again. It would have taken a man with a knife a long time to cut it.

"Right," said the mouse, and he began to chew.

Chew, chew, chew. One strand. Chew, chew, chew. Another. The crow stood guard and watched the path. The deer lay very still and tried not to worry. And the little mouse worked and worked, his sharp teeth going all night long.

Meanwhile, back at the lake, the tortoise was worried too. He could not fly, and he could not run, but he could not bear to sit still while his friend was in trouble.

So Mantharaka began to walk.

He walked through the reeds. He walked across the roots. He walked over stones and under ferns, slowly, steadily, one foot after another, all through the night, toward the edge of the forest where he knew the hunters set their nets.

Just as the sky was turning grey, the mouse chewed through the very last rope. The net fell away. Chitranga stood up, shaky at first, and then strong, and shook her ears.

She took one step, and then another, and then, because she could not help it, she leaped straight up into the air and came down again with all four feet at once, the way deer do when they are too happy to stand still. The mouse fell over laughing. Even the crow, who liked to look serious, hopped up and down on the grass.

"You did it!" said the crow.

"We did it," said the mouse, rubbing his sore jaw.

And at that very moment, the tortoise came pushing through the bushes, out of breath, if a tortoise can be out of breath. "I came as fast as I could," he panted. "Is she free? Oh, thank goodness."

But they had all been so busy being glad that they had not heard the footsteps on the path.

The hunter had come early.

He was a big man with a rough beard and a bag over his shoulder, and when he saw the empty net and the deer standing beside it, his mouth fell open. Chitranga sprang away into the trees. The crow shot up into the sky. The mouse vanished into a crack in the earth.

But the tortoise could not spring, or shoot, or vanish. He could only walk, slowly, and the hunter saw him.

"Well now," said the hunter. "A deer that got away, but a fine fat tortoise instead. That will do for my supper." And he picked up Mantharaka and put him in his bag and slung the bag over his shoulder and set off down the path toward his village.

From the trees, the three friends watched in horror.

"He has got Mantharaka!" squeaked the mouse. "We must save him!"

"He is too big to bite," said the crow, "and too strong to chase. We need a plan."

They thought. And the deer, who had been quiet, lifted her head.

"He wanted me," she said. "So let him think he can have me."

Chitranga ran ahead through the trees, faster than the hunter could walk. When she reached a place where the path passed close to a stream, she lay down on the bank with her legs sprawled out and her eyes half closed, as though she had fallen and hurt herself.

The crow flew down and stood on her side and pecked gently at her fur, the way crows do when they find something that cannot move.

Soon the hunter came along the path. He saw the deer lying by the stream. He saw the crow standing on her.

"Well, well," he said to himself. "The deer did not get far after all. It must have hurt itself running. Now I shall have a deer and a tortoise both."

He set down his heavy bag on the path, so that he could carry the deer more easily, and he walked toward the stream, rubbing his hands.

The moment the bag touched the ground, a small brown shape darted out of the ferns. It was the mouse. Chew, chew, chew went his teeth on the rope that tied the bag, and it fell open, and the tortoise crawled out and slid quietly into the stream.

The hunter reached the deer. He bent down to lift her.

And Chitranga leaped to her feet, quite unhurt, and bounded away up the bank in three great jumps. The crow flapped off with a caw that sounded very much like laughing.

The hunter spun around. His bag lay open and empty on the path. He looked at the stream, but there was nothing to see except ripples. He looked at the trees, but the deer was gone. He looked up, but the crow was only a black dot against the morning sky.

For a long moment he stood there scratching his beard. Then he shrugged, picked up his empty bag, and walked home to eat the rice and lentils his wife had cooked, which, to tell the truth, he liked much better anyway.

That evening the four friends met on the bank of the lake as usual. The deer had a scrape on one knee. The mouse had a very sore jaw. The tortoise was still a little damp. The crow had lost a feather somewhere. And none of them had ever been happier.

"You flew me through the sky," said the mouse to the crow.

"You chewed all night," said the deer to the mouse.

"You walked all night," said the crow to the tortoise.

"And you lay down in front of a hunter," said the tortoise to the deer, "which was the bravest thing of all."

Then they were quiet for a while, because good friends do not need to say everything.

The frogs began their evening song. The reeds rustled. One by one the stars came out over the little lake.

The crow tucked his head under his wing. The mouse curled up in a hollow root. The deer folded her long legs beneath her. And the tortoise, who had walked farther that night than in all his life, closed his eyes very slowly, and slept the deep and dreamless sleep of someone who has been a very good friend.
