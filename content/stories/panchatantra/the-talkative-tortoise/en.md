---
title: The Talkative Tortoise
summary: Two kind geese carry their tortoise friend to a new lake, if only he can keep his mouth closed for a little while.
moral: Sometimes the wisest thing to say is nothing at all.

Beside a quiet lake, under a leaning fig tree, lived a tortoise named Kambugriva. He had a shiny brown shell, a slow and steady walk, and one very big habit. He loved to talk.

He talked to the fish. He talked to the frogs. He talked to the reeds when nobody else was listening. Most of all, he talked to his two best friends, a pair of white geese who visited the lake every summer.

The geese did not mind. They liked him. "Kambugriva," they would say, "you tell the longest stories in the whole world." And the tortoise would smile and begin another one.

But one summer the rains did not come. The sky stayed blue and empty. Little by little the lake grew smaller, until it was hardly more than a puddle with a tortoise in the middle of it.

"We must fly to a bigger lake," said the geese. "There is one beyond the hills, deep and cool and full of lily pads."

"Take me with you," said Kambugriva. "Please. I cannot walk so far on these short legs."

The geese looked at each other. Then the first goose had an idea. "We will find a strong stick," she said. "We will each hold one end in our beaks. You will bite the middle, and hold on tight, and we will carry you through the sky."

"Wonderful," said Kambugriva.

"There is one thing," said the second goose gently. "You must not open your mouth. Not to talk, not to laugh, not to say hello. Not once, until we land."

"Of course not," said Kambugriva. "Not a single word. I promise."

So they found a stick, smooth and strong. The geese took the ends. The tortoise bit the middle. The geese flapped and flapped and flapped, and up they all went, over the fig tree, over the dry lake, and into the wide evening sky.

Oh, it was beautiful up there. The hills were soft and purple. The rivers were silver threads. The clouds were close enough to touch. Kambugriva had never seen anything so lovely, and he wanted very badly to say so. But he remembered his promise, and he kept his mouth shut tight.

They flew over a village. The children looked up and pointed. "Look!" they shouted. "A flying tortoise! Whoever saw such a silly thing?"

Kambugriva felt his cheeks grow hot. Silly? He was not silly. He was clever, and he had a hundred good things to say about it. The words rose up inside him like bubbles.

"I am not silly," he began.

And the moment his mouth opened, the stick slipped away. Down went Kambugriva, turning slowly in the air, his little legs waving.

But the geese had been watching him closely, because they knew their friend very well. They swooped after him, calling out, and steered him with their wings, this way and that, until he landed, with a soft whump, right in the middle of a farmer's haystack.

The hay was warm and dry and smelled of summer. Kambugriva lay in it for a moment, blinking. Nothing hurt. Only his pride was a little bruised.

The geese landed beside him. "Are you all right?" they asked.

"Yes," said the tortoise in a very small voice. "I opened my mouth. I am sorry."

"We know," said the geese kindly. "Shall we try again? Slowly, this time. And you may tell us everything you wanted to say once we are there."

So they found another stick. Kambugriva bit it firmly. And this time, all the way over the hills, he did not say a single word, though he thought a great many.

At last they came down beside a deep, cool lake, full of lily pads and dragonflies. Kambugriva slid into the water with a happy sigh.

Then he climbed onto a flat warm stone, tucked his head halfway into his shell, and told his friends the longest story in the whole world, until the geese fell asleep, and the frogs fell asleep, and finally, in the middle of a sentence, so did he.
