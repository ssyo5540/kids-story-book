---
title: The Blue Jackal
summary: A hungry jackal tumbles into a tub of blue dye, and for a little while the whole forest believes he is a king.
moral: It feels much better to be yourself than to pretend.

---

At the edge of a great green forest there was a small town, and at the edge of the town lived a jackal named Chandarava. He had a thin grey coat, a bushy tail, and a stomach that was very often empty.

One evening Chandarava crept into the town to look for supper. He sniffed at doorways. He peeked into gardens. But the dogs of the town saw him, and they began to bark, and Chandarava had to run.

He ran down one lane and up another. He ran through a gate and into the yard of a cloth dyer, and there, in the dark, he did not see the big tub of indigo dye. Splash! In he went, right up to his ears.

When he climbed out, dripping and shivering, he was not grey any more. He was blue. Blue from the tip of his nose to the tip of his tail, as blue as the evening sky just before the stars come out.

Chandarava crept back to the forest and hid until morning. But when the sun rose, the other animals saw him, and they stopped and stared.

"What is that creature?" whispered the deer.

"It is blue," said the monkeys. "Nothing in the forest is blue. It must be very special."

Now Chandarava had a clever, hungry sort of mind. He stood up tall and said in his grandest voice, "Do not be afraid. The sky has sent me to be your king. Bring me food, and I will look after you all."

And the animals believed him. The elephants fanned him with their ears. The tigers brought him the tastiest fruit and the softest leaves to sleep on. The peacocks danced for him every afternoon.

Only the other jackals were not welcome. "Go and live on the far side of the river," said Chandarava, because he was afraid that they would recognise him. And the jackals, sad and puzzled, went away.

For many days Chandarava ate and rested and was fanned and admired. But something was not right. At night, when the forest was quiet, he missed his brothers and sisters. He missed their silly games. He missed being just a jackal.

Then, one night, a full moon rose over the trees. Far away, on the other side of the river, the jackals lifted their heads and began to howl at it, the way jackals always do.

Chandarava heard them. His ears twitched. His nose pointed at the moon. And before he could stop himself, he threw back his head and howled too, a long, happy, jackal howl.

The animals woke up. They looked at their blue king. They looked at one another.

"Kings do not howl like that," said the deer softly.

"Only jackals howl like that," said the monkeys.

Chandarava's ears drooped. The moon shone on his blue coat, and it did not look grand any more. It just looked like paint.

"I am sorry," he said in a small voice. "I am not a king. I am only a jackal who fell into a tub of dye. I was hungry, and then I was afraid to tell the truth."

The animals were quiet for a moment. Then the old elephant knelt down so that his kind eyes were level with the jackal's.

"We are glad you told us," he said. "Go home to your family, little one. And next time you are hungry, just ask."

So Chandarava crossed the river. His brothers and sisters ran to meet him, and they laughed at his blue fur, and they rolled him in the soft dust until most of it came off.

That night the jackals curled up together in a warm heap. The moon climbed high. And somewhere in the middle of the heap, a slightly blue jackal fell asleep, happier than any king.
