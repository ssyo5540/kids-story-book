title: The Monkey and the Crocodile

moral: A good friend tells the truth, even when it is hard.

On the bank of a slow brown river there grew a rose apple tree, so old and so wide that its branches leaned right out over the water. The fruit was small and dark and purple, and it was the sweetest fruit for a hundred miles.

In the tree lived a monkey named Raktamukha. He had a red face, a long tail, and a very kind heart. All day he swung through the branches, eating rose apples and dropping the pips into the river with a little plop.

One afternoon a crocodile came swimming slowly by. His name was Karalamukha, and he was long and green and looked rather fierce, but he was tired and hot, and he climbed up onto the sandy bank below the tree to rest.

Raktamukha looked down at him. Karalamukha looked up. For a moment neither of them said anything.

Then the monkey did something friendly. He picked the ripest rose apple he could find and dropped it. It landed with a soft thump right in front of the crocodile's nose.

Karalamukha sniffed it. He nibbled it. His eyes went wide. "Oh," he said. "Oh, that is good."

"Have another," said the monkey, and dropped one more.

That was how it began. Every afternoon after that, the crocodile swam to the rose apple tree, and the monkey dropped fruit, and the two of them talked. The monkey told stories about the treetops, about birds and squirrels and the way the wind sounded in the leaves. The crocodile told stories about the river, about the fish and the rain and the deep cool places where the water was green.

They became the best of friends. And the crocodile was so happy that every evening he carried a few rose apples home in his mouth for his wife.

When the monsoon came and the river rose brown and roaring, a baby monkey from a nearby tree slipped from a wet branch and tumbled into the water. Raktamukha screamed. But before he could even climb down, Karalamukha was there, gliding under the little one and lifting him gently onto his broad back, and carrying him to the bank as carefully as a boat carries an egg.

"You saved him," said Raktamukha, shaking.

"You feed me every day," said the crocodile, as if that explained everything.

And once, when a fisherman came quietly along the bank with a wide net, it was the monkey high in the tree who saw him first, and shrieked and threw rose apples until the crocodile woke and slid away safely into the deep water. So the two of them had already saved each other once each, which is a very good beginning for any friendship.

Now the crocodile's wife lived in a muddy hollow on the far bank, and she was not a cheerful crocodile. She was grumpy in the morning and grumpy at night, and the more her husband talked about his friend in the tree, the grumpier she grew.

"Monkey this, monkey that," she snapped one evening. "You are never home. You spend all day laughing under a tree with a monkey."

"He is my friend," said Karalamukha. "Try a rose apple. They are wonderful."

She ate one. It was wonderful. That made her even grumpier.

"If the fruit he eats is this sweet," she said slowly, "then the monkey himself must be sweeter still. Bring him to me. I want to gobble him up for dinner."

Karalamukha stared at her. "He is my friend," he said again.

"Your friend!" she cried. "A crocodile with a monkey for a friend. Whoever heard of such a thing? Bring him here tomorrow, or I will never speak to you again." And she turned her back on him and went to sleep.

The crocodile did not sleep at all that night. He lay in the mud and looked at the moon and felt sick to his stomach.

The next afternoon he swam to the rose apple tree very slowly. The monkey dropped a fruit as usual, but Karalamukha did not eat it.

"Friend," he said, "my wife has heard so much about you that she would like to meet you. She has made a feast. Will you come home with me for dinner? I will carry you on my back across the river."

Raktamukha was delighted. He had never been across the river, and he had never been to a feast. He scrambled down the tree and hopped onto the crocodile's broad green back.

"Wait, wait," he said, and scrambled back up again. He picked the twelve finest rose apples on the whole tree, the darkest and the sweetest, and he held them bunched in his tail like a bouquet of flowers. "For your wife," he explained, climbing back down. "One does not go to a feast empty-handed."

Karalamukha looked at the little bouquet, and something in his chest hurt.

"Hold on," said Karalamukha, and he slid into the water.

The river was wide and brown and slow. The bank with the rose apple tree grew smaller behind them. The far bank was still a long way ahead. And in the middle of the river, where the water was deepest, the crocodile stopped swimming.

"Why have we stopped?" asked the monkey.

Karalamukha did not answer at first. Then all at once the words came tumbling out of him, because he could not carry them any longer.

"I have not been honest with you," he said. "My wife does not want to meet you. She wants to eat you. She thinks that you must be sweeter than the fruit. I did not know what to do. I am so sorry."

Raktamukha sat very still on the crocodile's back. The water lapped around them. Far away a fish jumped.

He was frightened. But he was also a clever monkey, and even more than that, he was a kind one. So instead of shouting, he thought.

"Oh, my dear friend," he said at last, in a voice as calm as the river. "Why did you not tell me sooner? Of course your wife wants my heart. Everyone knows a monkey's heart is the sweetest thing in the world. But I do not carry it around with me."

"You do not?" said the crocodile.

"Certainly not," said Raktamukha. "It might fall out while I am jumping. I keep it safe in a hollow of the rose apple tree. If you had only told me, I would have brought it along. Turn around, and we will fetch it."

Karalamukha was so relieved that he did not stop to wonder. He turned in the water and swam back the way they had come, as fast as his tail could push him.

As they went, the monkey kept talking, because talking kept him calm. "It is quite a small heart," he said. "About the size of a rose apple, actually, so your wife may be disappointed. Perhaps she would like these instead." And he held out the bouquet in his tail. "Take them home to her, whatever happens. They are the best I have."

The crocodile swam faster. The rose apple tree grew bigger and bigger in front of them, and to Raktamukha it looked like the most beautiful thing in the world.

The moment his nose touched the sandy bank, Raktamukha leaped. Up the trunk, into the branches, to the very top of the tree. He sat there with his tail wrapped tightly around a branch and his heart, which had been in his chest the whole time, beating very fast indeed.

The crocodile waited on the sand. "Have you got it?" he called.

The monkey looked down. He was safe now. He could have laughed at his friend, or scolded him, or thrown pips at his head.

Instead he said gently, "Karalamukha. A monkey keeps his heart in his chest, like everybody else. I said that to get home safely. But you told me the truth in the middle of the river, when it would have been easy to say nothing. So I think your heart is a good one, even if it got a little lost today."

The crocodile hung his head. Two large tears rolled down his long green nose and dripped onto the sand.

"I nearly did a terrible thing," he said.

"Nearly," said the monkey. "But you did not. That is the part that counts."

Karalamukha swam home, very slowly, and told his wife everything. And when she saw how sad he was, and how ashamed, something in her grumpy heart went soft, just a little. She never asked for the monkey again. Some say she even started eating rose apples herself, and grew a bit less grumpy, one fruit at a time.

The crocodile still came to the rose apple tree every afternoon. But now he stayed on his side of the sand, and the monkey stayed in his branches, and they talked across the space between them, which is a perfectly good way for a monkey and a crocodile to be friends.

Every evening the monkey dropped a few extra rose apples, and Karalamukha carried them home in his mouth.

And when the sun went down and the river turned to gold, Raktamukha curled up in his favourite fork of the old tree, with his heart safe inside him where it belonged, and fell asleep to the sound of the water going by.
