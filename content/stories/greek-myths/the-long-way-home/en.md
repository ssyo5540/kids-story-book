## The King Who Wanted to Go Home

Far out in the blue sea, where the water is so clear you can count the fish, there is a small rocky island called Ithaca.

It is not a rich island. It has goats and olive trees and a harbour shaped like a cupped hand. But the people who live there love it more than any palace, and none of them ever loved it more than its king, whose name was Odysseus.

Odysseus was not the tallest king or the strongest. He was the cleverest. He could think his way around a problem the way water finds its way around a stone. He had a wife called Penelope, who was cleverer still and did not mind him knowing it, and a baby son called Telemachus, who had just learned to grab his father's beard and hold on.

Then came the war.

It was a long quarrel between kingdoms very far away, and I will not tell you all of it, because it is not a story for the end of the day. It is enough to say that Odysseus did not want to go, and went anyway, because he had promised, and that he kissed the top of his son's head and told Penelope, "Ten years at the most," and meant it, and was wrong.

The quarrel lasted ten years all by itself. And it was Odysseus, in the end, who found a way to finish it, with a wooden horse and a very clever idea, and when it was over, all the kings gathered their ships and turned toward home.

Odysseus had twelve ships and six hundred men, and on the morning they set sail, he stood at the front of the first ship with the wind in his beard and the sea ahead of him, and he said out loud, "Ithaca."

He thought it would take a few weeks.

Now, the sea belonged to the god Poseidon, who was moody and enormous and easily offended, and there were other gods, too, who took an interest in mortals the way children take an interest in ants. Some of them liked Odysseus. Athena, the grey-eyed goddess of wisdom, liked him very much, because he thought before he acted, which was rare. But others were watching for him to make a mistake.

And Odysseus, being human, made several.

The first few days were easy. Blue water, fair wind, dolphins racing the ships. The men sang. Odysseus stood at the prow and thought about goats and olive trees and a small hand grabbing his beard.

Then, on the fourth night, the wind changed. It came out of the north, cold and wild, and it blew the twelve ships south for nine days and nine nights, far off any map, to a shore that nobody recognised.

Odysseus climbed a hill and looked out at strange trees and a strange sun, and he understood, with a sinking feeling, that the way home was not going to be short.

"Well," he said to himself. "Then we shall have to be clever."

And the sea was quiet that night, and Ithaca was far away.

## The Lotus-Eaters and the Giant

The first strange land was full of people who smiled too much.

They were gentle folk who lived on the fruit of a plant called the lotus, and the lotus made you sleepy and happy and forgetful. Three of Odysseus's men went ashore to ask for water, and the lotus-eaters gave them fruit, and the men ate it, and sat down under a tree, and smiled, and forgot that they had ever wanted to go anywhere at all.

Odysseus had to carry them back to the ships himself, one over each shoulder, with the third one wandering behind him picking flowers. He tied them to their benches until the fruit wore off, and they woke up cross and embarrassed and very hungry.

"Nobody eats anything on a strange island," said Odysseus, "unless I have eaten it first."

The second strange land was worse.

It was an island of green hills where enormous sheep grazed, and in a cave above the sea lived a giant. He had one eye in the middle of his forehead and a voice like rocks rolling downhill, and his name was Polyphemus.

Odysseus and twelve men had gone into the cave to look for food. They found cheeses the size of cart wheels and buckets of milk, and they were just deciding how much they could carry when the floor shook and the giant came home, driving his sheep in ahead of him. He rolled a boulder across the cave mouth to shut them all in, and then he turned his single eye on the small men standing among his cheeses.

"Visitors," he said, pleased. "How nice. I shall keep you."

He did not mean it kindly. Giants are not kind. He picked up two of the men and shook them and laughed and put them in a wicker basket hanging from the ceiling, the way you might keep birds, and said he would decide what to do with them tomorrow.

Then he lay down across the cave mouth and fell asleep with a snore that shook dust from the ceiling.

Odysseus did not sleep. He sat in the dark and thought.

In the morning the giant drove his sheep out to graze, rolling the boulder aside just long enough to let them through and rolling it back again before any small man could follow. All day Odysseus thought. When the giant came home at dusk, Odysseus stepped forward with a big wooden bowl in his hands.

"Great one," he said politely, "we have brought you a gift from our ship. Wine. Have you tasted wine?"

Polyphemus had not. He drank the whole bowl in one gulp and smacked his lips and held it out for more. He drank a second bowl. He drank a third. His single eye began to droop.

"What is your name, little man?" he rumbled. "I like you. I shall keep you longest."

"My name," said Odysseus, "is Nobody."

"Nobody," said the giant, and laughed until he hiccupped, and fell asleep so hard that he slid down the wall.

Odysseus and his men worked all night. They lifted the two men down from the basket. And when the giant rolled the boulder aside at dawn to let out his sheep, every single one of the small men was hanging underneath a sheep, clinging to the thick wool with both arms, so that the giant felt the sheep's backs as they passed, one by one, and never once thought to feel underneath.

They ran for the ship. The giant heard them and roared and came stumbling down the hill, but he could not see them well in the bright morning, and the other giants who came running to help shouted, "Who is bothering you?" and Polyphemus bellowed, "NOBODY!" and the other giants shrugged and went home.

The ships pulled away. And Odysseus, standing at the stern, could not resist.

"Giant!" he shouted across the water. "It was not Nobody. It was Odysseus of Ithaca who tricked you! Remember the name!"

Polyphemus hurled a rock the size of a house that landed just behind the ship and soaked everyone. And then he lifted his face to the sky and called out to his father, who was Poseidon, god of the sea, and asked him to make the way home very long indeed for a certain boastful king.

Poseidon heard.

"That," said one of the men, wringing out his cloak, "was perhaps not your cleverest moment."

"No," admitted Odysseus. "It was not."

And the sea was quiet that night, but it was listening now, and Ithaca was farther away than ever.

## The Bag of Winds and the Witch's Island

The next island belonged to a king named Aeolus, who was in charge of all the winds of the world and kept them in a cave like horses in a stable. He was cheerful and generous and he liked Odysseus at once.

"Take this," he said when it was time to leave, and he handed over a great leather bag tied with a silver cord. "I have put every wind in the world inside it except the soft west wind, which will blow you straight to Ithaca. Do not open the bag. Whatever you do."

For nine days the west wind blew them home. On the tenth morning Odysseus, who had not slept the whole way because he was so afraid of missing something, saw a shape on the horizon. Rocky. Small. A harbour like a cupped hand.

Ithaca.

He could see the smoke of the cooking fires. He sat down on the deck and, for the first time in ten years, closed his eyes.

While he slept, the men looked at the leather bag.

"He got a present from the wind king," said one. "Look how heavy it is. Gold, probably. And he does not share."

"Just a peek," said another.

They untied the silver cord.

Every wind in the world burst out at once. The ships spun like leaves. The sea stood up in hills. And the storm blew them away from Ithaca, away from the smoke of the cooking fires, back across every mile they had come, and dropped them, battered and soaked, on the beach of the wind king's island.

Aeolus came down to look at them and shook his head. "I cannot help you again," he said sadly. "Something is against you. You had better find out what."

Odysseus said nothing at all for a whole day.

Then he got up and began to think again, because that was what he did.

The next island was quiet and green, with a house of polished stone in the middle of a wood, and around the house wandered wolves and lions that were far too gentle, rubbing against the men's legs like cats. Inside lived a woman named Circe, who was a witch.

She invited the men in. She gave them cheese and honey and wine. And when they had eaten, she tapped each of them on the shoulder with a wand, and they turned into pigs.

Fat, pink, contented pigs, with the men's own eyes looking out of their faces. She put them in a pen with acorns to eat, and to tell the truth some of them rather enjoyed it, because acorns are delicious to a pig and nobody expects a pig to row.

One man had waited outside, and he ran back to the ship and told Odysseus.

Odysseus set off for the stone house alone. On the path he met a young man with winged sandals, who was Hermes, the messenger of the gods, sent by Athena.

"Eat this," said Hermes, and gave him a small white flower. "It will keep the magic from touching you. Then be firm with her, but not unkind. She is lonely, mostly. Lonely people do strange things."

So Odysseus ate the flower and knocked on the door. Circe gave him wine. She tapped him with the wand. And nothing happened.

She stared at him. He looked back at her steadily.

"You must be Odysseus," she said at last. "Hermes told me one day someone would come who could not be changed. Very well. What do you want?"

"My men," he said. "Back on two legs, please. And a bath. And a rest. We are very tired."

Circe laughed, and it was not a witch's laugh, only a woman's. She went out to the pen and touched each pig, and they stood up as men, blinking, and one of them said, "I was just getting used to it."

They stayed on Circe's island for a whole year. She fed them and healed their hurts and mended their sails, and she and Odysseus talked long into the evenings about the shapes of the stars. When at last he said, "I have to go home," she did not try to stop him.

"The way home," she said, "passes the singing rocks. Listen carefully. This is what you must do."

And the sea was quiet that night, and Ithaca was a memory, and a hope.

## The Singing Rocks

There are rocks in the sea where creatures called Sirens live, and the Sirens sing.

Their song is not loud. It is not even beautiful, exactly. But whoever hears it wants, more than anything in the world, to stop the ship and go and sit on the rocks and listen forever. Sailors who hear it never go home. They simply sit, and listen, and forget.

"Fill your men's ears with wax," Circe had said, "so they hear nothing. But you, Odysseus, you will want to hear it. You are that sort of man. So have them tie you to the mast, and tell them that whatever you say, whatever you beg, they are not to untie you until the rocks are far behind."

Odysseus did exactly that. He melted a wheel of wax and pressed it into every ear on the ship. Then he stood against the mast while they wound the rope around him, and he said, "Row. Do not stop. Do not look at me."

They rowed. The rocks came into view, grey and low, and on them sat the Sirens, and they began to sing.

Odysseus heard it.

It was a song about home. About a small rocky island with goats and olive trees. About a woman at a loom, and a boy growing tall, and a harbour shaped like a cupped hand. "Stay," the song said. "You are so tired. Stay here, and we will sing you home every day, and you will never have to row again."

He strained against the ropes. He shouted at the men to untie him. He promised them gold, and then he begged, and then he wept. The men, hearing nothing, kept their eyes on their oars and rowed harder, exactly as they had been told.

The rocks slid behind. The song faded. And Odysseus sagged in the ropes, wet-faced and shaking, and when they finally untied him he sat on the deck for a long time.

"What did it sound like?" asked one of the men, pulling the wax from his ears.

"Like home," said Odysseus. "That is what makes it dangerous."

Beyond the rocks the sea narrowed between two cliffs. Under one cliff was a whirlpool called Charybdis, which sucked the whole sea down three times a day and spat it up again in a fountain that touched the clouds. On the other cliff lived a creature called Scylla, with many long necks and many heads, who liked to snatch things off passing ships.

"You cannot avoid both," Circe had warned. "Choose the cliff. Row fast, and hold on to everything."

They rowed fast. They held on. The whirlpool roared on one side, and Scylla's heads came down on the other, snapping, and the ship shot between them like a stone from a sling, and when they came out into open water and counted, they had lost one oar and a barrel of apples and nothing else at all.

"Well," said Odysseus, a little pale. "I liked those apples."

Then came the island of the Sun.

It was covered in fat, beautiful cattle, and the cattle belonged to the Sun god, who loved them, and Odysseus made every man swear not to touch them. "We have bread. We have fish. Leave the cattle alone." They swore.

But a storm kept them on the island for a month, and the bread ran out, and the fish stopped biting, and one evening while Odysseus slept, the men broke their promise. They took the cattle. They ate them. They felt very sorry afterwards, and it did not help.

The Sun saw. The Sun told Zeus. And when the ship sailed at last, Zeus sent a storm like the end of the world, and the ship broke apart under it, and the sea took everything.

Odysseus woke in the dark, alone, clinging to a broken mast.

He drifted for nine days. He saw no land. He saw no ship. He thought about a woman at a loom and a boy who would be nearly twenty now, and he held on, because that was the last thing he knew how to do.

On the tenth day, the mast bumped against sand.

And the sea was quiet at last, and Ithaca had never seemed so far.

## Seven Years with Calypso

The island was small and green and smelled of cedar, and it belonged to a goddess named Calypso.

She found him on the beach, half-drowned and tangled in weed, and she carried him to her cave, which was hung with vines and warm with a fire, and she nursed him back to health. She was beautiful and kind and very lonely, and she fell in love with him before he had properly woken up.

"Stay," she said, when he was well. "Stay here with me. You will never grow old. You will never be cold or hungry or afraid. You will never have to row again."

It was the Sirens' song, but gentler, and truer, and offered with love.

Odysseus thanked her. And he stayed, because he had no ship, and no way to build one, and because he was so very tired.

He stayed for seven years.

He was not unhappy, exactly. Calypso was good to him. The island was lovely. But every single morning he walked down to the shore and sat on the same rock and looked east, toward where Ithaca must be, and every single evening Calypso found him there, and every single evening she said nothing, and led him back to the fire.

Far above, on her high mountain, Athena had not forgotten him.

"Father," she said to Zeus, "he has been punished enough. Poseidon is still sulking, but even a sea god must forgive eventually. Let him go home."

Zeus considered. Then he called Hermes.

Hermes flew down to the cedar island in his winged sandals and found Calypso weaving at her loom, and he gave her Zeus's message as gently as he could.

Calypso did not cry in front of him. She waited until he had gone. Then she went down to the shore, where Odysseus sat on his rock looking east, and she sat down beside him.

"You may go," she said. "The gods say so. I would have kept you forever, and you would never have been sad, and you would never have been sick. But you would never have been home, either. I see that now."

Odysseus took her hand.

"You have been kinder to me than I deserved," he said. "I will remember this island for as long as I live."

"Longer than that, I hope," said Calypso, and she smiled, and wiped her eyes, and helped him build a raft.

It took four days. She showed him the best trees. She gave him rope and sailcloth and bread and wine and water. On the fifth morning she stood on the shore and raised a soft warm wind, and the raft went out over the waves, and she watched until it was gone.

Poseidon, of course, saw him.

The sea god had been sulking for seventeen years, which is a long time even for a god, and when he saw the small raft bobbing along toward Ithaca he lost his temper one last time. The sky went black. The waves rose. The raft came apart plank by plank, and Odysseus was in the water again, swimming, for two days and two nights, until a river mouth on a strange shore took him in, and he crawled up under a bush of olive leaves and slept like a stone.

He was woken by a ball landing on his head.

A princess named Nausicaa and her friends were playing on the beach, and they screamed when a wild bearded man sat up out of the bushes, all except Nausicaa, who did not scream, and who looked at him and saw that he was more frightened than she was.

"You look like you need help," she said.

Her father was king of that island, and the people there were sailors, the best in the world. They fed Odysseus and gave him clean clothes and, after he had told them his whole long story, they gave him a ship.

He fell asleep on the deck before it left the harbour. The sailors rowed all night. And in the grey of dawn they lifted him, still sleeping, and laid him down on a beach, with gifts piled around him, and rowed quietly away.

When he woke up, he did not know where he was.

Then he saw the harbour. It was shaped like a cupped hand.

And the sea was quiet, and Ithaca was under his feet.

## The Stranger in the Hall

He did not run to the palace.

Twenty years is a long time, and Odysseus was clever, and he wanted to know what he was walking into. So when Athena came down to the beach in the shape of a shepherd boy and told him the news, he listened carefully.

The news was not good.

Penelope had waited. For twenty years she had waited, and for the last ten, when everyone else had given him up for lost, a crowd of loud young lords had moved into the palace and refused to leave. They ate her food. They drank her wine. They told her every day that Odysseus was gone and she must marry one of them.

Penelope, being cleverer than all of them together, had said, "Certainly. As soon as I have finished weaving this cloth." And every day she wove, and every night she crept down and unpicked what she had woven, so that the cloth was never finished, and this had gone on for three years before one of the maids gave her away.

And Telemachus, the baby who had grabbed his father's beard, was a young man now, tall and worried, trying to hold the palace together and not knowing how.

Odysseus listened to all this with his jaw tight.

"Let me help," said Athena, and she touched him, and he became an old beggar with a bent back and a grey beard and rags, so that nobody in Ithaca would know him.

The first one who knew him was a dog.

An old, old hound lay on a dung heap by the palace gate, too weak to stand, with a grey muzzle and a thin tail. His name was Argos. Odysseus had trained him as a puppy, twenty years before. And as the old beggar shuffled past, the dog lifted his head, and his ears went up, and his tail moved, once, twice, in the dust.

Odysseus could not stop. He could not bend down. Anyone might be watching. He wiped his eye with the back of his hand and walked on, and behind him Argos laid his head down again, quite content, because he had waited long enough and now he had seen.

Inside the great hall the loud young lords were feasting. They threw bones at the old beggar. They laughed at him. He bowed his head and took it and watched them all, one by one, and remembered every face.

Telemachus, though, was kind to the stranger. He gave him bread and a seat by the door. And that night, in a quiet room, Athena let the disguise slip for a moment, and Telemachus looked at the old beggar and saw his father's face, and the two of them held each other and did not say much, because there was too much to say.

"Tell no one," said Odysseus. "Not yet. Not even your mother. We must be clever."

There was one more who knew him. An old nurse called Eurycleia, who had rocked him as a baby, was washing the beggar's feet that evening, and she saw a scar on his knee from a boar hunt long ago, and she dropped the basin with a clang and opened her mouth, and Odysseus put a finger to his lips, and she closed it again and wept silently into her apron.

The next day, Penelope announced a contest.

"Here is my husband's great bow," she said to the loud young lords. "Whoever can string it and shoot an arrow through twelve axe rings in a row, I will marry."

One by one they tried. The bow would not bend. They oiled it and warmed it and heaved at it and went purple in the face, and it stayed straight as a stick.

Then the old beggar by the door said, "May I try?"

They laughed until they cried. Telemachus said, "Let him."

Odysseus took the bow. He turned it over in his hands the way you hold an old friend. He bent it, and it bent, easily, and he slipped the string over the end, and he plucked the string and it sang, and every lord in the hall went silent.

He fitted an arrow. He shot. It went through all twelve rings and buried itself in the far wall.

Then he stood up straight, and the rags fell away, and Athena filled the hall with a light so bright and so strange that the loud young lords could not look at it, and they saw, in the middle of the light, the king of Ithaca with his bow in his hand.

They ran. They ran for the doors and the windows and the harbour and their ships, and they did not stop running until they were home in their own kingdoms, and none of them ever came back to Ithaca or spoke of that day again.

The hall was empty. The floor was covered in bones and spilled wine. And in the doorway stood Penelope.

And the sea was quiet, and Ithaca was home, and still the story was not finished.

## The Olive Tree

Penelope did not run to him.

She had been fooled before. Twenty years of strangers claiming news of Odysseus, of hopes raised and dropped, had made her careful. She stood in the doorway and looked at the tall man with the bow, and her face gave nothing away.

"You may be my husband," she said. "Or you may be a god playing a trick. I have to be sure. Eurycleia, move the great bed out of our room, and make it up in the hall for our guest."

Odysseus stared at her.

"Move it?" he said. "Nobody can move that bed. I built it myself, around the trunk of a living olive tree that grew in the courtyard. I built the room around the tree, and the bed around the trunk, and I carved the posts and inlaid them with silver and stretched the oxhide straps with my own hands. It has roots. It cannot be moved. Unless someone has cut down my tree."

Penelope's knees gave way.

Nobody in the whole world knew about the olive tree except the two of them. She had asked the one question that only her husband could answer, and he had answered it, cross and confused and exactly himself.

She crossed the hall and put her arms around his neck and held on, and Odysseus held her, and Telemachus discovered that he needed very badly to go and check on something in the courtyard.

They talked all night.

She told him about the loom and the loud young lords and the long, long waiting. He told her about the giant and the witch and the singing rocks and the seven years on the cedar island. He told her about the mast, and the nine days in the water, and the rock where he had sat every morning looking east.

"Every morning?" said Penelope.

"Every morning."

Athena, who was watching from her mountain and who was, underneath everything, a soft-hearted goddess, reached out and held back the dawn, so that the night could go on a little longer and they could finish talking.

In the morning, when the sun finally rose, Odysseus walked out into the hills to find his father.

Old Laertes was in his orchard, on his knees in the dirt, digging around the roots of a pear tree. He was very old now, and thin, and his clothes were patched, and he did not look up when the stranger came through the gate.

Odysseus stood and watched him for a while. Then he said, "That tree has grown. You gave it to me when I was a boy. Thirteen pear trees, ten apple, forty figs, and this one was the smallest. You told me the names of all of them."

The old man's trowel dropped into the dirt.

He did not say his son's name. He could not. He just held out his arms, and Odysseus knelt down in the orchard beside him, and the two of them stayed there among the pear trees for a long time while the bees went about their business.

Ithaca was quiet after that. Truly quiet. The loud young lords were gone. The palace was swept. The goats grazed on the hills and the olive trees grew, and Telemachus married a girl from the next valley, and Argos the old dog was buried under the pear tree with a stone to mark the place.

Poseidon, they say, got over his sulk in the end. Athena spoke to him, and Zeus spoke to him, and mostly, twenty years is long enough even for a sea god to hold a grudge. The waters around Ithaca were calm ever after, and the fishermen came home every evening with full nets, and nobody was ever again blown off the edge of the map.

And Odysseus?

Odysseus grew old in the house he had built around a tree. He walked down to the harbour most mornings, out of habit, and looked at the sea. But he did not look east any more. He was already east. He was already home.

Sometimes, in the evenings, his grandchildren climbed into his lap and asked for the story of the giant with one eye, and he told it, and they asked for the pigs, and he told that too. And when they asked, "Was it worth it, Grandfather? All those years?" he would look at Penelope across the room, and at Telemachus in the doorway, and at the firelight on the walls of his own house, and he would say, "Every wave of it."

Then he would carry them up to bed.

So close your eyes. You are on a small rocky island, in a house built around a living tree. The harbour is shaped like a cupped hand. The goats are asleep on the hills, and the olive leaves are whispering, and somebody who was gone a very long time is home.

And the sea is quiet, and Ithaca is here.
