---
title: King Midas and the Golden Touch
summary: A king who wishes that everything he touches would turn to gold discovers, by breakfast time, exactly what gold cannot do.
moral: The best things in life are the ones gold cannot buy.
reviewStatus: needs_review
---

Long ago, in a kingdom of rolling hills and rose gardens, there lived a king named Midas.

Midas was not a bad king. He was kind to his servants and fair to his farmers, and he loved his little daughter, whose name was Zoe, more than all the jewels in his treasury. But Midas had one weakness. He loved gold. He loved the shine of it, the weight of it, the soft yellow glow of it in the lamplight. He counted his gold coins every evening and wished, every evening, that there were more.

One morning his gardeners found an old man asleep among the roses.

The old man had a round red face and a beard full of leaves. He had wandered away from a party the night before and lost his way. The gardeners wanted to chase him off, but Midas said, "No. Bring him inside. Give him breakfast. Everyone gets lost sometimes."

So the old man ate a very large breakfast and told a very great many stories, and Midas laughed until his sides hurt. Then the king gave him a warm cloak and had him driven home in the royal chariot.

Now it happened that the old man was a friend of the god Dionysus, and Dionysus was so pleased to have him back safe that he came to the palace himself, in a swirl of grapevines, to say thank you.

"Midas," said the god, "you were kind to my friend when you did not have to be. Ask me for anything, and it shall be yours."

Midas did not have to think for long. The wish had been sitting in his heart for years.

"Let everything I touch," he said, "turn to gold."

Dionysus looked at him for a moment, and there was something sad in his eyes. "Are you sure?" he asked.

"Quite sure," said Midas.

"Then it is done," said the god, and he was gone in a rustle of leaves.

Midas stood alone in his hall. He hardly dared to try. Then, slowly, he reached out and touched the back of a chair.

The chair turned to gold. Smooth, gleaming, solid gold.

Midas gasped. He touched a table. Gold. He touched a curtain, and it stiffened into a sheet of gold that clanged softly against the wall. He ran out into the garden and touched a rose, and every petal turned to thin gold leaf that chimed in the breeze.

"It is true!" he shouted. "It is true!"

He ran through the palace like a boy, touching everything. Pillars. Doors. The stairs under his feet. By the time the sun was high, the whole palace glittered, and Midas was breathless and delighted and hungrier than he had ever been in his life.

He sat down at the golden table for lunch.

He picked up a piece of bread. It turned to gold in his fingers, heavy and hard and cold. He set it down and picked up a grape. Gold. He lifted a cup of water to his lips, and the water turned to liquid gold and then to solid gold, and he could not drink it.

Midas stared at the golden bread. He was very, very hungry. And for the first time since the god had gone, a small worried feeling began to grow in his chest.

That was when Zoe came running in.

She was six years old, with tangled hair and grass on her knees, and in her arms she carried her kitten, a small grey thing called Smoke that followed her everywhere.

"Papa!" she cried. "The roses are all yellow and hard! And they do not smell any more! What happened to the roses?"

"I..." said Midas. "It was..." He did not know how to explain.

Zoe held out the kitten so that he could see it. "Smoke is frightened," she said. "Pet him, Papa. He likes it when you pet him."

And Midas, who had petted that kitten a hundred times, reached out without thinking.

The kitten turned to gold.

It happened all at once. One moment Smoke was warm and soft and purring. The next he was a small golden statue in Zoe's arms, heavy and still, with his tail curled and his eyes closed.

Zoe looked down at him. Her face crumpled. And she began to cry, the way children cry when something they love has been broken.

Midas fell to his knees. He wanted to hold her, to comfort her, to wipe her tears. But he did not dare touch her. He did not dare touch his own daughter.

"I am sorry," he whispered. "Oh, Zoe, I am so sorry. I have been so foolish."

He looked around at his shining palace, at the golden bread and the golden roses and the golden kitten, and he understood, all at once, what gold could not do. It could not be eaten. It could not smell sweet. It could not purr. And it could not, ever, be hugged.

"Dionysus!" he cried, lifting his face to the ceiling. "I was wrong. Please. Take it back. I do not want it. I only want my daughter's kitten to be warm again."

The air filled with the smell of grapes, and the god was there.

"Well," said Dionysus gently, "that did not take long. Most people need a week."

"Please," said Midas.

"Go to the river Pactolus," said the god, "where it comes down from the mountain. Wash your hands in the water. Then bring some of the water back and sprinkle it over everything you have touched."

Midas ran. He did not stop for a horse or a chariot. He ran out of the palace and through the golden garden and down the long road to the river, with Zoe running behind him, still holding the little golden cat.

The river Pactolus was cold and fast and clear. Midas knelt on the bank and plunged his hands into the water, right up to the elbows.

For a moment nothing happened. Then the gold began to flow out of him. It streamed from his fingers in bright ribbons and swirled away downstream, and where it touched the bottom, the river sand turned yellow and glittering. And Midas felt lighter, as if he had set down something heavy that he had been carrying for years.

He lifted his hands out. They were only hands. Wet and cold and ordinary.

He filled a jug from the river and hurried home. He sprinkled water on the roses, and they softened and blushed pink and filled the garden with their smell again. He sprinkled the bread, and it was bread. He sprinkled the chairs and the tables and the curtains, and the palace was just a palace once more, with a few scratches on the floor.

And then, with Zoe standing close beside him, he sprinkled a few drops of river water on the little golden kitten.

Smoke shivered. His fur turned grey. His tail twitched. He opened his eyes, gave an enormous yawn, and jumped out of Zoe's arms to go and chase a butterfly, exactly as if nothing had happened.

Zoe laughed and laughed. Then she flung her arms around her father's neck, and Midas hugged her back with all his strength, and it was the best thing he had ever felt in his whole life.

From that day on, Midas did not count his coins in the evening. He counted other things instead. Roses in the garden. Stars over the hill. The number of times his daughter laughed at dinner, which was usually a great many.

And the river Pactolus still runs down from the mountain with gold in its sand, glittering in the shallows, so that anyone who kneels to drink from it can see, just under the water, a king's whole fortune, and walk away lighter for leaving it there.
