Long ago, before there were seasons, the world was always warm.

Flowers bloomed all year round. Wheat waved gold in every field. Trees were never bare, and nobody had ever seen snow.

All of this was thanks to Demeter, the goddess of growing things. Wherever she walked, green shoots pushed up through the soil. And walking beside her, nearly always, was her daughter Persephone.

Persephone loved flowers more than anything. She knew the name of every one. She could make a daisy open just by smiling at it. When she laughed, poppies turned to look.

Now, far below the fields where Persephone gathered her flowers, there was another kingdom. It was a quiet place, cool and dim, lit by lamps and glowing stones. It was the land where people went when their long lives were over, to rest.

The king of that kingdom was called Hades. He was not cruel and he was not frightening, whatever anyone said about him later. He was only very quiet, and very serious, and very, very lonely. Nobody ever laughed in his halls. Nothing ever grew there.

One day, Hades looked up through a crack in the earth and saw Persephone in a meadow, filling her arms with flowers, with the sun in her hair. And he thought that he had never seen anything so alive.

He went to Zeus, the king of the gods, who was his brother.

"Brother," he said, "my kingdom is so quiet. I have no one to talk to. I would like Persephone to come and be my queen and light up my halls."

Zeus, who did not always think things through, said, "Very well." He did not ask Persephone. He did not ask Demeter. That was the first mistake, and everything that followed grew out of it.

So one afternoon, while Persephone knelt in a meadow picking a narcissus with a hundred petals, the ground opened beside her with a soft rumble. A dark chariot rose up, drawn by four black horses, and in it sat Hades.

"Come and see my kingdom," he said, and before Persephone could answer, the chariot swept her up and sank back into the earth, and the meadow closed over them like water.

Persephone was not hurt. But she was very far from home, and she had not said goodbye.

Up above, Demeter came looking for her daughter. She found the basket of flowers, tipped over in the grass. She found the narcissus, still fresh. But she could not find Persephone anywhere.

"Persephone!" she called across the meadow. No answer came.

Demeter searched the whole world. She searched every field and forest and mountainside. She asked the rivers. She asked the wind. She asked the sun, who sees everything, and at last the sun told her the truth.

She walked so far that her sandals wore through. She did not eat. She did not sleep. In a village by the sea, an old woman offered her a bowl of soup, and Demeter was so tired that she sat down on the doorstep and cried, and the old woman patted her hand and said nothing, which was exactly the right thing to say.

"Hades has taken her below," he said gently. "Zeus gave his blessing."

Demeter sat down on a stone and did not move.

And because she did not move, nothing grew.

The wheat stopped waving. The flowers closed and drooped. The leaves on the trees turned yellow and brown and, for the first time ever, let go and fluttered to the ground. The air grew cold. And then, one morning, something soft and white came drifting down from a grey sky, and the animals looked up in wonder, because none of them had ever seen snow.

The whole world was waiting, the way a mother waits.

Meanwhile, under the earth, Persephone walked through Hades' quiet halls. It was strange there, but it was not terrible. The lamps glowed like small moons. The rivers ran slow and silver. And Hades was kind to her, in his serious way. He showed her gardens of pale silver flowers that never needed sunlight. He gave her a throne beside his own. He listened when she talked, which nobody in that kingdom had ever done before, and slowly, the halls began to feel a little less silent.

But Persephone missed the sun. She missed her mother. She missed green things, and warm wind, and bees. She would not eat, because she did not want to belong to that place. Every day Hades set fruit before her, and every day she shook her head.

The spirits who lived in the quiet kingdom noticed her, though. They had been resting there so long that they had forgotten the taste of apples and the feel of grass. When Persephone walked past, humming an old song from the meadows, they lifted their heads. A little colour came into their faces. One of them, a grandmother who had been sitting still for a hundred years, smiled.

"Tell us about the sun," they asked her, and she did. She told them about bees and barley and the smell of rain on a dusty road. And Hades, listening from his dark throne, understood for the first time why he had wanted her there. It was not her beauty. It was that she remembered the world, and helped everyone else remember it too.

Up above, Zeus looked down at the frozen world and finally understood what he had done. The fields were empty. The people were cold and hungry. And Demeter still sat on her stone, waiting.

So Zeus sent for Hermes.

Hermes was the fastest of the gods, with winged sandals and a quick smile, and he could go anywhere, even under the earth. "Go to Hades," said Zeus, "and bring Persephone home. Tell him it is my wish, and tell him I am sorry."

Hermes flew down through the crack in the world and into the quiet halls, and he found Persephone sitting by a silver river, holding a pomegranate in her lap.

"I have come to take you home," he said. "Your mother is waiting."

Persephone stood up so quickly that the pomegranate rolled away. Then she stopped.

"I have eaten," she said in a small voice.

"Eaten?"

"I was so hungry. Hades opened this pomegranate for me, and the seeds were so red, and I ate six of them. Only six."

Hermes looked at Hades, and Hades looked at the floor. It was an old rule, older than the gods themselves, that whoever eats the food of the kingdom below belongs to it, at least a little.

"Six seeds," said Hermes slowly. "Then here is what we will do."

He turned to Hades. "She has eaten six seeds. For six months of the year she will stay here with you and be your queen, and your halls will not be silent. For the other six she will go home to her mother and the sun. Is that fair?"

Hades was quiet for a long time. He looked at Persephone, who had made his kingdom feel like a home. He knew that keeping her would only make her sad, and that a sad queen would be no better than an empty throne.

"It is fair," he said at last. And then, softly, "Will you come back?"

Persephone looked at him. At the lamps like moons. At the silver flowers. At the loneliest king in all the worlds, who had been kind to her in his own quiet way.

"I will come back," she said. "In the autumn, when the leaves fall. And I will bring you stories from the sun."

Then Hermes took her hand, and they rose up through the earth together.

The first thing Persephone saw was her mother, sitting on a stone in a field of snow. Demeter looked up. And the moment she saw her daughter, she leaped to her feet, and the snow melted where she stepped, and green shoots came pushing up through the mud, and every tree along the road burst into blossom at once.

That was the first spring.

Demeter and Persephone held each other for a long time. Then they walked together across the fields, and everywhere they walked the world woke up.

And so it has been ever since.

For half the year Persephone lives in the sun with her mother, and the world is warm and green and full of growing things. That is spring, and summer.

Then, when the leaves begin to turn, she kisses her mother goodbye and goes down to the quiet kingdom to sit beside Hades and tell him stories. The world grows cold and still. Demeter waits. That is autumn, and winter.

But every winter ends. That is the promise. Snow melts. The ground softens. And one morning a single flower pushes up through the cold earth, because Persephone is coming home.

Tonight, wherever you are, the earth is slowly turning toward one season or another. The trees know it. The birds know it. Somewhere, a goddess is on her way somewhere she is loved.

Close your eyes. Winter is only waiting. Spring always comes back.
