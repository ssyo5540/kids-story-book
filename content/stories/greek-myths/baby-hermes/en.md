title: Baby Hermes' Busy First Day
summary: A brand-new baby god climbs out of his cradle, invents music, plays a trick on his big brother, and makes a friend, all before bedtime.
moral: A kind gift can turn a quarrel into a friendship.

In a cave on a quiet mountain, early one morning, a baby was born. His mother, Maia, wrapped him in a soft blanket, kissed his nose, and laid him in his cradle. Then she lay down to rest, because babies are tiring, even for goddesses.

The baby's name was Hermes. And Hermes had no intention of staying in his cradle.

Before the sun had even climbed over the hills, he wriggled out of his blanket, stood up on his brand-new legs, and toddled to the mouth of the cave to have a look at the world.

The first thing he saw was a tortoise, ambling slowly across the grass with its shell shining in the early light.

"Good morning," said Hermes, who had been born knowing how to talk. "What a beautiful house you carry."

The tortoise, who was very old and rather vain, was so pleased to be admired that he lent Hermes his shell for the afternoon, the way magic tortoises can.

Hermes turned the shell over. It was round and hollow and it hummed when he tapped it. He stretched seven pieces of string across it, tight, from one edge to the other. Then he brushed his fingers over them.

A sound came out. A sweet, ringing, shimmering sound that nobody in the world had ever heard before, because nobody in the world had ever made a lyre.

Hermes laughed with delight. He played another note, and another, and then a tune.

But by afternoon the baby was restless again.

Over the hill, he could hear cows.

They belonged to his big brother Apollo, the golden god of the sun, who had fifty fine white cows grazing in a meadow. Hermes toddled over the hill to look at them. He thought they were wonderful. And he thought it would be very funny to borrow them.

So he did. He led all fifty cows away, walking them backwards so that their hoofprints pointed the wrong way, and he tied little brooms of grass to their tails so that their tracks were swept away behind them. Then he hid them in a cave, kissed each one on the nose, and toddled home. He climbed back into his cradle, pulled up his blanket, and pretended to be a very small, very innocent baby.

By evening, Apollo came looking. He was tall and bright and rather cross.

"Someone has taken my cows," he said to Maia. "And the only new person on this whole mountain is that baby."

"That baby," said Maia, "was born this morning."

Apollo looked into the cradle. Hermes gazed up at him with big round eyes and sucked his thumb.

"You," said Apollo.

"Me?" said Hermes, in the smallest voice imaginable.

Apollo lifted him out of the cradle and carried him up to their father, Zeus, on his high mountain. Zeus listened to the whole story. But when he looked at the tiny baby who had already invented music and stolen fifty cows before bedtime, his beard began to twitch, and then he burst out laughing.

"Give your brother his cows back," he said at last, wiping his eyes. "And then make friends. That is an order."

So Hermes led Apollo to the cave, and all fifty cows came trotting out, quite happy, with the grass brooms still tied to their tails. But Apollo was still frowning a little. So Hermes fetched the tortoise-shell lyre, sat down on a rock, and began to play.

The music floated out over the evening. It was gentle and golden and new. Apollo, who loved beautiful things more than anything in the world, stopped frowning. Then he sat down. Then he closed his eyes.

"What is that?" he whispered when the song ended.

"I made it," said Hermes. "It is for you."

He put the lyre into his brother's hands. Apollo held it as if it were made of starlight.

"For me?" he said.

"To say sorry about the cows," said Hermes. "And because you would play it better than I ever could."

Apollo plucked one string, and then another, and a smile spread over his face like sunrise. From that day on, the lyre was Apollo's, and he became the greatest musician there has ever been, and he never, ever forgot who gave it to him.

The two brothers walked home together in the dusk, the tall golden one and the very small one, and they were friends for the rest of forever.

Hermes climbed back into his cradle. His mother tucked the blanket around him. Somewhere over the hill, Apollo was playing a slow, soft tune, the very first lullaby.

And the busiest baby who ever lived closed his eyes at last, on the very first night of his very first day, and fell fast asleep.
