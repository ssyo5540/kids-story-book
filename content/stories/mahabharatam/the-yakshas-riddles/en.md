title: The Yaksha's Riddles by the Lake
summary: One by one, four thirsty brothers fall asleep beside a magical lake, until the eldest stops to listen to the voice in the reeds and answer its riddles.
moral: Listening first is the wisest kind of strength.

---

It was the hottest day of the whole summer. The five Pandava brothers had been walking through the forest since sunrise, and their water pots were empty, and their throats were dry as dust.

At last they sat down in the shade of a big neem tree. Yudhishthira, the eldest, looked around at his brothers. Their faces were red. Their lips were cracked.

"We must find water," he said. "Nakula, you are the quickest. Climb that tree and see if there is a lake nearby."

Nakula climbed. From the top branch he shaded his eyes and looked in every direction. "There!" he called down. "Not far to the east, I can see the shine of water, and cranes flying over it."

"Go and fill the pots," said Yudhishthira. "We will wait here."

Nakula ran through the trees, and soon he came out on the bank of the most beautiful lake he had ever seen. The water was clear as glass. White lotuses floated on it. Tall reeds swayed at the edge, and a single grey crane stood among them on one leg.

It was so quiet there that Nakula could hear his own heart. No frogs croaked. No birds sang. Even the wind seemed to have stopped at the edge of the trees and decided not to come any closer. If he had not been so thirsty, he might have noticed that this was strange.

Nakula knelt down and reached for the water.

"Wait," said a voice.

Nakula looked around. There was nobody there. Only the crane, watching him with a bright black eye.

"Answer my questions before you drink," said the voice. "That is the rule of this lake."

But Nakula was so thirsty that he could not think about rules. He scooped up the cool water in both hands and drank and drank. And the moment the water touched his lips, a great sleepiness came over him. He lay down on the soft grass of the bank and closed his eyes.

Under the neem tree, the brothers waited. And waited.

"He has been gone too long," said Sahadeva at last, and he went to look for his brother.

He found the lake. He found Nakula asleep on the bank. He heard the voice say, "Answer my questions before you drink." But he was so worried and so thirsty that he drank first, meaning to find out what had happened after. And he, too, lay down and slept.

Then Arjuna went. Arjuna, the great archer, who never missed. He saw his two brothers sleeping, and he lifted his bow, and he shot arrows into the reeds where the voice came from, but the arrows only rustled the leaves. And the voice said again, "Answer my questions before you drink."

"Whoever you are," said Arjuna, "you cannot stop me." He drank. And the sleep came down on him like a soft, heavy blanket.

Then Bhima went, the strongest of them all. He saw three brothers sleeping and he roared with worry and stamped up and down the bank. "Come out and face me!" he shouted at the reeds. Nothing came out. So Bhima drank the water in great gulps, and he lay down beside the others with a sigh like a falling tree.

Now only Yudhishthira was left under the neem tree, and he was very much afraid.

He walked slowly to the lake. And when he saw his four brothers lying on the bank, so still and so quiet, his heart nearly stopped. He knelt beside each of them. They were breathing. They were warm. They were only asleep, but it was a sleep he could not wake them from.

Yudhishthira looked at the clear water. He was thirstier than he had ever been in his life. He reached toward it.

"Wait," said the voice. "Answer my questions before you drink."

And Yudhishthira did something that none of his brothers had done. He took his hand back from the water. He sat down on the bank. And he listened.

"Who are you?" he asked politely.

"I am a Yaksha," said the voice, "a spirit of this lake. Your brothers would not listen to me. But you have stopped. Will you answer my riddles?"

"I will try," said Yudhishthira. "Please ask them."

The reeds rustled, and the crane tilted its head, and the questions began.

"What is faster than the wind?"

Yudhishthira thought. "A thought," he said. "You can think of a faraway place before the wind has crossed the garden."

"Good," said the voice. "What is heavier than the earth?"

"A mother's love for her children," said Yudhishthira. "It carries more than any mountain."

"Good. What is higher than the sky?"

"A father's hopes for his children."

"What is the one thing that grows bigger the more you give it away?"

Yudhishthira smiled. "Kindness."

"What is the best of all treasures?"

"A calm mind," said Yudhishthira. "With it, you are rich in a hut. Without it, you are poor in a palace."

"What can cut without a blade?"

"An unkind word," said Yudhishthira, "and it leaves a deeper mark."

"What fills a whole house without taking up any room?"

Yudhishthira closed his eyes and thought of his mother's kitchen, long ago, in the palace. "Laughter," he said. "And light. Both of them."

"What is the best medicine in the world?"

"Sleep," said Yudhishthira, and he looked at his brothers, lying so peacefully on the grass. "Everything looks better in the morning."

"Who is truly rich?"

"The one who is happy with what he has. Wanting more is a kind of poverty, even for a king."

"And what is the greatest gift a parent can give a child?"

Yudhishthira was quiet for a long moment. He thought about his father, who had gone to rest among the stars when he was very young. He thought about his mother, walking with them through every forest.

"Time," he said. "Just time. Sitting with them. Listening to them. Nothing else lasts the way that does."

"And what is the truest friend a person has?"

"The good they have done," said Yudhishthira softly, "because it stays with them wherever they go."

The lake was very quiet. Even the lotuses seemed to be listening.

"You have answered well," said the voice at last. "Better than anyone who has ever come to my lake. I will grant you a gift. You may choose one of your brothers, and I will wake him."

Yudhishthira looked at the four sleeping figures. He loved every one of them. Arjuna, who could shoot a bird's eye from a mile away. Bhima, who could lift an elephant. Sahadeva, who knew the stars. Nakula, who was gentle with every horse and every stranger.

"Wake Nakula," he said.

"Nakula?" said the voice, surprised. "Not Arjuna, with his bow? Not Bhima, with his strength? Those two would be more useful to you."

"I have two mothers," said Yudhishthira. "My own mother, Kunti, and my other mother, Madri, who went to rest among the stars long ago. Arjuna and Bhima and I are Kunti's sons. Nakula and Sahadeva are Madri's. If I can wake only one, it is fair that each mother should have one son awake. Please wake Nakula."

For a long moment there was no sound at all. Then the reeds parted, and the crane stepped out of them, and as it stepped, it changed.

It grew tall. Its feathers became a robe. Its bright black eye became two eyes, deep and gentle and full of light. And Yudhishthira found himself looking at a great and kindly being with a face he had seen only in dreams.

"I am not a Yaksha," said the being. "I am Dharma, the spirit of what is right. And I am your father, Yudhishthira. I made this lake, and I sent the thirst, and I asked the riddles, because I wished to see what kind of man you had become."

Yudhishthira bowed his head all the way to the grass.

"You did not grab," said Dharma. "You did not shout. You did not shoot arrows at what you did not understand. You stopped, and you listened, and you were fair even when fairness cost you something. That is worth more than all the strength in the world."

He lifted his hand. And on the bank, Nakula yawned and stretched. Then Sahadeva. Then Arjuna. Then Bhima, who sat up and said, "Is there anything to eat?"

Nakula rubbed his eyes and looked around in confusion. "I only had one sip," he said. "Did I fall asleep?"

"For a little while," said Yudhishthira, smiling.

Sahadeva looked at the crane-shaped footprints in the mud and then at the tall shining figure on the bank, and being the cleverest of the brothers, he understood at once and bowed low. Arjuna picked up his arrows one by one from among the reeds and did not say anything, but his cheeks were a little red.

Dharma laughed. The lake laughed with him, in little sparkling waves.

"Drink now, all of you," he said. "The water is only water. And remember your brother, who listened."

Then he was gone, and there was only a grey crane, lifting out of the reeds and flying slowly away over the trees.

The five brothers knelt at the edge of the lake and drank. The water was cool and sweet, the sweetest they had ever tasted. They filled their pots. They washed the dust from their faces.

And then, because the day had been long and the shade was deep, they lay down together on the soft grass, all five of them in a row, the way they had slept as children.

The lotuses closed their petals for the night. The frogs began to sing. And the stars came out over the lake, one by one, until the whole sky was full of quiet answers.
