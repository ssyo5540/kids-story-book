## The Princess Who Chose

Once, in the forest, when the five Pandava brothers were feeling very low, the old sage Markandeya sat down beside Yudhishthira and said, "Let me tell you about a princess who would not give up." And this is the story he told.

There was a king in the land of Madra who had no children. He was a good king, and he wanted a child very much, and so for many years he prayed to the goddess of the dawn, whose name is Savitri. At last a daughter was born to him, and because she had been a gift from the dawn, he called her Savitri too.

She grew up bright and quick and kind. She could ride a horse as well as any prince. She could argue with the palace teachers until they laughed and gave in. She noticed things: a servant who looked tired, a bird with a hurt wing, a beggar at the gate who had not eaten. And she did something about them, every time, without being asked.

When Savitri was old enough to marry, her father did an unusual thing. Instead of choosing a husband for her, as kings did in those days, he called her to him and said, "You have always known your own mind better than I have. Take a chariot and some guards and go and see the world. When you find someone you would like to spend your life with, come back and tell me."

So Savitri went.

She travelled for many months. She saw cities and villages and mountains and rivers. She met a great many princes, and some of them were handsome, and some of them were clever, and none of them were quite right.

Then one afternoon her chariot came to the edge of a great forest, and she got down to stretch her legs, and she heard the sound of an axe.

She followed the sound. In a clearing she found a young man cutting wood. He was tall and strong and sunburnt, and his clothes were made of rough cloth, and he was singing to himself while he worked. When he saw her he stopped, and bowed, and smiled the sort of smile that goes all the way up to the eyes.

"You are a long way from anywhere," he said. "Are you lost? Come and drink some water."

His name was Satyavan. He lived in a small hut in the forest with his mother and father. His father had once been a king, but he had lost his sight, and while he was blind his enemies had taken his kingdom, and now the old man and his wife lived on roots and berries and whatever their son could gather, and they were, Satyavan said, quite content.

Savitri stayed for three days. She helped the old blind king find his way to the stream. She ground grain with his wife. She watched Satyavan carry water for his parents, and tell them stories in the evening, and laugh, and she understood that she had found what she had been looking for.

She went home and told her father.

Now it happened that the sage Narada was visiting the palace that day, and Narada, who wandered between heaven and earth and knew nearly everything, went pale when he heard the young man's name.

"Satyavan is a good man," he said slowly. "The best you could find. But there is something you must know. His name is written in the great book of Yama, the keeper of names. One year from today, Yama will come to the forest to walk him home. He has only one year to live."

The king turned to his daughter. "Savitri," he said gently. "You heard. Choose again. There are other good men."

Savitri was quiet for a long moment. Then she lifted her head.

"I have chosen once," she said. "A heart is not a market where you pick one thing and then put it back for another. One year or a hundred, I choose Satyavan."

Narada looked at her for a long time. Then he did something unexpected. He smiled.

"Then go," he said, "and be happy. And Savitri, when the day comes, do not be afraid of Yama. He is not what people think."

So the princess married the woodcutter, and gave away her silks, and went to live in a hut in the forest. And that night, lying awake and listening to the trees, she began, very quietly, to count the days.

## A Year in the Forest

The year was the happiest of Savitri's life. That is the strange part, and the true part.

She learned to wake before the sun. She learned to build a fire with damp wood and to find honey in a hollow tree. She led the blind old king to the stream each morning and described the light on the water to him, so that he could see it in his mind.

"Silver today," she would say. "With a little gold where the sun comes through."

"Ah," he would say. "I remember silver."

Satyavan taught her the names of the birds. She taught him the names of the stars, which she had learned from the palace teachers, and in the evenings they lay in the clearing and pointed them out to each other until they fell asleep.

She never told him about the year. She thought about it every single day, and she never said a word. It sat inside her like a stone in a pocket, and she carried it, and she was kind anyway, and cheerful anyway, and she laughed at his jokes, which were not always very good.

Once, in the cold season, the old queen fell ill with a fever, and Savitri sat up with her for three nights, bathing her forehead with cool water and telling her stories from the palace until the fever broke. "You did not have to marry into a hut," the old woman whispered on the third night. "You could have had anything."

"I have what I chose," said Savitri. "That is better than anything."

The old queen held her hand and did not say anything more, and in the morning she was well.

The seasons turned. The rains came and went. The leaves fell and grew back.

When only four days were left, Savitri stopped eating. She stood in the clearing from sunrise to sunset without moving, and she prayed, not loudly, not with tears, but the way you hold a door shut against a wind, steadily, with your whole body.

"You are worrying about something," Satyavan said on the third evening. "You have been worrying for weeks. Tell me."

"I am making a promise to myself," said Savitri. "When it is kept, I will tell you everything."

On the morning of the last day, Satyavan picked up his axe.

"I am going to cut wood," he said. "We are nearly out."

"I am coming with you," said Savitri.

"You have not eaten in three days. Stay and rest."

"I am coming with you," she said again, in a voice he had not heard before, and he looked at her, and something in him understood that this was not a day to argue.

So they went into the forest together.

It was a beautiful morning. Sunlight fell through the leaves in long gold bars. Satyavan found a dead tree and began to chop, and Savitri sat on a fallen log nearby with her hands folded, watching him, memorising him: the way he swung the axe, the way he pushed his hair off his forehead, the way he hummed between breaths.

Toward midday he stopped.

"That is strange," he said. "I feel so tired all of a sudden. And my head aches." He put down the axe. "Let me sit with you a moment."

He came and lay down in the grass with his head in her lap. Savitri stroked his hair. The birds went on singing. And slowly, gently, the way a lamp burns down at the end of an evening, Satyavan's breathing grew slow, and slower, and his eyes closed.

Savitri did not cry. She had been getting ready for this moment for three hundred and sixty-five days.

She looked up. And there, standing at the edge of the clearing, was Yama.

## The Quiet Walk

He was not at all what people said.

They said the keeper of names was terrible to look at. But Yama was tall and dark and very still, with kind eyes that had seen everything and were not surprised by any of it. He wore a plain robe. He carried a small book. Beside him stood a great black buffalo with patient eyes, and the forest was perfectly quiet around them, as if even the birds knew to be respectful.

"Savitri," said Yama. His voice was deep and slow, like a bell heard from far away. "I know you. Narada told me you would be here. I am sorry. It is time for Satyavan to walk home with me."

He came across the clearing and bent over the sleeping man, and from Satyavan's chest he lifted something. It was small, no bigger than a thumb, and it glowed like a candle flame, and it was the part of Satyavan that was really Satyavan. Yama held it carefully in his palm.

"Go back to the hut," he said to Savitri, quite kindly. "Look after his parents. That is your task now."

He turned and walked away into the trees, south, toward the place where the sun goes.

Savitri laid Satyavan's head down gently on the grass. She stood up. And she followed.

She walked behind Yama through the forest, keeping a few steps back, not speaking. After a while Yama stopped and turned around.

The path went downhill, always downhill, and the light grew softer, the colour of late evening even though it was only afternoon. The trees changed too. They were taller here, and older, and they stood very still, as if they were listening. Savitri's bare feet made no sound on the moss. Ahead of her, the small light in Yama's palm glowed steadily, and she kept her eyes on it and did not look anywhere else.

"Child," he said. "You cannot come this way. This is a road for those whose names I have called. Go home."

"My lord," said Savitri, "in my father's house they taught me that a wife walks beside her husband wherever he goes. You are carrying my husband. So I am walking beside him. I am only doing what I was taught."

Yama looked at her. Something moved in his face that might have been surprise.

"You speak well," he said. "For that, I will give you a gift. Ask me for anything, except the life I am carrying."

Savitri did not hesitate. "My husband's father is blind," she said. "Give him back his sight."

"It is done," said Yama. "Now go home."

He walked on. She followed.

After a while he stopped again. "Savitri. You are still here."

"My lord," she said, "the wise say that walking even seven steps with a good person makes you their friend. I have walked more than seven steps with you. So we are friends now, and one does not leave a friend on a lonely road."

Yama was quiet for a long moment. The buffalo swished its tail.

"That is very good," he said at last, and there was something almost like a smile at the edge of his mouth. "Another gift. Anything except the life."

"My husband's father lost his kingdom," said Savitri. "Give it back to him."

"It is done. Go home."

He walked on. She followed.

The forest grew darker. The path grew narrower. Savitri's feet hurt, and she had not eaten in three days, and she did not stop.

Yama stopped a third time. He turned. He looked at the small tired princess in her rough forest clothes, who had followed the keeper of names farther than anyone in all his long, long memory.

"Why?" he asked. Not as a rebuke. As a question. "You know I cannot give him back. Why do you keep walking?"

Savitri thought about it. She wanted to give him a true answer.

"Because you are kind," she said. "Everyone says you are frightening, but you have given me two gifts and asked for nothing. Kind people can be talked to. So I am talking to you. And because I promised to walk beside him. And because," and here her voice shook for the first time, "I am not finished being happy yet."

Yama held the small glowing light in his palm and looked at it.

"One more gift," he said very quietly. "Anything except the life."

Savitri took a breath. She had thought about this moment for a year, and she knew exactly what to say.

"Then give me this," she said. "Give me many children with Satyavan. Sons and daughters, a whole houseful, growing up strong and good in a home with their father in it."

"It is done," said Yama, without thinking, because he was moved, and because he was used to saying it.

Then he stopped.

He looked at the light in his hand. He looked at Savitri. And Savitri looked back at him with her chin up and her eyes steady and just the smallest, smallest twinkle in them.

"You cannot have children with Satyavan," said Yama slowly, "unless Satyavan is alive."

"No, my lord," agreed Savitri. "You cannot."

"And I have promised."

"You have, my lord."

For a moment the whole forest held its breath.

Then Yama laughed.

It was not a loud laugh. It was a deep, slow, delighted rumble, like far-off thunder on a summer night, and the buffalo looked round in astonishment because it had never heard that sound before.

"Savitri," said Yama, "in all the ages of the world, nobody has ever walked beside me this far, and nobody has ever talked me out of a name. You did not do it with tears. You did not do it with anger. You did it with patience, and wisdom, and a very clever question. I am not even sorry."

He opened his hand. The small light rose from his palm and floated back through the trees, the way they had come, glowing softly in the dark.

"Go after it," said Yama. "He is waiting. And Savitri. Live a long time. I shall not be back for either of you for many, many years."

He bowed to her, the keeper of names bowing to a princess in rough cloth, and he turned and walked away south with his patient buffalo, and the forest closed behind him.

Savitri ran.

## Home

She found him where she had left him, lying in the grass in the clearing, with the sunlight moving over him. The little light drifted down and settled on his chest and sank into it like water into sand.

Satyavan stirred. He yawned. He opened his eyes and looked up at her.

"Did I fall asleep?" he said. "I had the strangest dream. There was a tall man, and a path, and someone walking behind us the whole way, and I was not afraid, because I knew who it was." He sat up and rubbed his face. "How long was I sleeping? It is nearly dark."

"Long enough," said Savitri. And then, because she had kept it inside her for a whole year and could not keep it one moment longer, she put her arms around him and told him everything.

He listened without a word. When she had finished he was quiet for a long while.

"You knew," he said. "All year. You knew, and you stayed."

"Of course I stayed."

"And you followed him. Into the dark. On foot. With no supper."

"I told you I was making a promise to myself," said Savitri. "That was the promise."

They walked home through the forest in the last of the light, holding hands, going slowly, because Savitri's feet really did hurt very much now that she had time to notice.

Halfway there Satyavan stopped and picked her up, because she was limping, and carried her the rest of the way like a bundle of firewood, and she protested the whole time, and did not mean a word of it.

When they came to the hut, the old king was standing in the doorway, and he was looking at them. Looking. His eyes were clear and bright and full of tears.

"I can see the fire," he said in wonder. "I can see the trees. Satyavan, my boy, is that your face? I had forgotten your face."

Before anyone could answer, there was a sound of horses on the forest road, and torches, and a crowd of men came riding into the clearing and threw themselves down before the old king. They were his people. The enemies who had taken his kingdom had quarrelled among themselves and gone. The throne was empty, and the people had come to find their rightful king and bring him home.

The old man wept. His wife wept. Satyavan looked at Savitri, and Savitri looked at the ground and tried very hard to seem surprised.

"You," said Satyavan.

"I only asked," said Savitri.

They went home to the kingdom, all of them, in the morning. The blind king ruled again with clear eyes for many years, and then Satyavan ruled after him, and beside Satyavan, always, was Savitri, who noticed things and did something about them.

And the house filled up with children, exactly as Yama had promised, a whole noisy houseful of them, and Savitri lived a very long time, long enough to tell this story to every one of her grandchildren, and to see them fall asleep before the end, the way children do.

"So you see," said the old sage Markandeya to Yudhishthira, in the forest, long after, "that is what patience looks like. It does not shout. It walks."

Yudhishthira lay back on the grass and looked at the stars for a long time.

The fire burned low. The brothers slept. Somewhere in the dark, very far away, a tall quiet figure with a patient buffalo went about his work, and was in no hurry, and thought, now and then, about a princess who had made him laugh.
