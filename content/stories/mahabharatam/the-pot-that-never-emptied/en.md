title: The Pot That Never Emptied

moral: A small gift shared with love can fill everyone up.

Once, for a long while, the five Pandava brothers lived in the forest instead of a palace. They slept under trees. They washed in rivers. And they had very little to eat.

Their wife, Draupadi, worried about this most of all. Every evening travellers and wise old teachers wandered into their camp, tired and hungry, and Draupadi hated to send anyone away with an empty stomach.

So one morning she stood by the river at sunrise and asked the sun for help. The sun listened. And a copper pot came floating down through the golden light and settled gently in her hands.

"This pot will never be empty," said the sun, "until you yourself have eaten your last mouthful of the day. Feed everyone first, and eat last, and there will always be enough."

Draupadi did exactly that. Every evening she cooked, and the pot gave rice and dal and warm bread and sweet fruit for as many guests as came. Bhima, the biggest brother, ate more than anyone, and still the pot was never empty. Only when Draupadi had eaten her own supper did the pot fall quiet until morning.

Now one evening, after everyone had eaten and Draupadi had taken her last mouthful, a famous old teacher named Durvasa arrived with a great many students. Durvasa was known everywhere for being very, very grumpy when he was hungry.

"We have come for dinner," he announced. "We will bathe in the river first, and then we will eat." And off they went to the water.

Draupadi looked at the copper pot. It was empty, and it would stay empty until dawn. She looked at Yudhishthira, the eldest brother, and he looked at her, and neither of them knew what to do.

So Draupadi sat down quietly under a tree, closed her eyes, and thought about her dear friend Krishna. "If you can hear me," she whispered, "please come."

And there he was, standing in the firelight with his gentle smile, as if he had simply been out for an evening walk.

"Draupadi," said Krishna, "I am so hungry. Have you got anything at all to eat?"

"Nothing," she said, and her eyes filled with tears. "The pot is empty, and a hundred hungry guests are coming."

"Let me see the pot," said Krishna.

He turned it over and peered inside. Stuck to the very bottom, where no one had noticed it, was one small grain of rice with a tiny piece of spinach leaf.

Krishna picked it up carefully, as if it were a jewel, and ate it. Then he sat back and sighed happily, like someone who has just finished a wonderful meal.

"Ah," he said. "Now I am full."

Down by the river, something strange happened. Durvasa and his students had finished their bath and were drying themselves, when suddenly, all at once, they felt completely full. Their stomachs were round and warm, as though they had eaten a great feast.

"I could not eat another thing," said one student.

"Nor I," said another.

Durvasa rubbed his beard. He was too full even to be grumpy. "Then we must not trouble that kind family tonight," he said. "Come. We will thank them another day." And they walked away down the river path, yawning.

Back at the camp, Krishna laughed his quiet laugh. "You see," he told Draupadi, "when one grain is given with a whole heart, it fills the whole world."

Draupadi wiped her eyes and smiled. Bhima yawned an enormous yawn. The fire burned down to soft red coals.

And the copper pot sat in the moonlight, quite empty and quite content, waiting for the sun to come up again.
