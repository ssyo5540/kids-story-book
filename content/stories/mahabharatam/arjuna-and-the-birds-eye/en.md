title: Arjuna and the Bird's Eye
summary: Teacher Drona sets a quiet test for his students high up in a tree, and young Arjuna discovers what it means to see only one thing.
moral: When your mind is calm, you can see clearly.

Long ago, in a kingdom called Hastinapura, there lived five brothers. People called them the Pandavas. They were still boys, and every morning they walked together to the edge of the forest, where their teacher waited under a tall old tree.

Their teacher's name was Drona. He was a quiet man with kind eyes and a long grey beard. He taught the boys how to hold a bow, how to breathe slowly, and how to wait. Waiting, he said, was the hardest lesson of all.

He never raised his voice. When a boy made a mistake, Drona simply asked him to try again, and then again, until the mistake grew tired and went away by itself.

One evening, as the sun turned the sky the colour of ripe mangoes, Drona climbed a little way up the tall tree. He placed a small wooden bird on a high branch, where the leaves were thick and the light was soft.

"Tomorrow," he said gently, "we will see who can find the bird's eye."

"Yes, Guruji," said the boys. Then they walked home under the first stars, ate their supper, and slept.

In the morning the forest was cool and full of birdsong. Drona called the eldest brother first. Yudhishthira was wise and careful. He lifted his bow and looked up into the tree.

"What do you see?" asked Drona.

"I see the tree, Guruji," said Yudhishthira. "I see the branches and the leaves. I see the wooden bird, and I see you, and I see my brothers." Drona smiled and asked him to lower his bow. "Rest now," he said. "You have seen a great deal."

Then came Bhima, the strongest of the brothers. Bhima was big and warm and always a little hungry. He looked up and said, "I see the bird, and the branch, and a squirrel, and a very nice mango." Drona laughed softly and asked him to rest too.

One by one the boys looked up, and one by one they saw many things. The sky. The leaves. Each other. Drona thanked each of them and asked each of them to sit down in the shade.

At last it was Arjuna's turn. Arjuna was not the oldest and not the strongest. But he loved his bow the way some children love a blanket, and he had listened very carefully to every lesson about waiting.

Arjuna lifted his bow. He took one slow breath in, and one slow breath out. The forest seemed to grow quiet around him, the way a room grows quiet when a lamp is turned low.

"What do you see?" asked Drona.

"I see the eye of the bird," said Arjuna.

"Do you see the tree?" asked Drona. "Do you see the leaves? Do you see your brothers?"

"No, Guruji," said Arjuna. "Only the eye."

Drona did not say anything for a moment. Then he put his hand on Arjuna's shoulder. "That," he said, "is how you see when your mind is calm. Everything else fades away, and only the one small thing remains."

Arjuna lowered his bow and smiled. He did not need to shoot. The wooden bird sat safely in its tree, and the brothers sat together in the shade, and the sunlight came down through the leaves in little golden coins.

That night, when the five brothers lay down to sleep, Arjuna looked out at the dark sky. There were so many stars. He chose one small star, and he looked only at that one, and his breathing grew slow and soft.

The forest was quiet. The wooden bird was quiet. And one by one, the brothers closed their eyes, and the whole sky kept watch over them until morning.
