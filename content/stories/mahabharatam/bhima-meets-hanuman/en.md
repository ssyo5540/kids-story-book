---
title: Bhima Meets Hanuman in the Forest
summary: The strongest of the Pandava brothers sets out to find a golden flower and finds instead an old monkey whose tail he cannot move.
moral: Real strength is gentle.
reviewStatus: needs_review
---

High in the mountains, where the air is cool and the pine trees whisper, the five Pandava brothers made their camp. It was the time of their long stay in the forest, and every day was an adventure, though not always a comfortable one.

One morning Draupadi was walking by the stream when the wind brought her something wonderful. A flower came tumbling through the air and landed at her feet. It was a lotus, but not an ordinary lotus. It was golden, and it had a thousand petals, and it smelled like honey and sunshine and rain all at once.

Draupadi picked it up and held it to her face and closed her eyes. "Oh," she said. "I have never smelled anything so lovely. I wish I had a whole armful of these."

Bhima heard her. Bhima was the second brother, and the strongest by far. He was as tall as a door and as wide as two, and he loved Draupadi and his brothers so much that he would do anything to make them happy.

"Where did it come from?" he asked.

"The wind brought it," said Draupadi, "from somewhere up there." And she pointed to where the mountains climbed into the clouds.

"Then I will go up there," said Bhima, "and I will bring you back a hundred."

He picked up his great mace, which was made of iron and as heavy as a cart, and he set off up the mountain with his big strides.

Now Bhima was not a quiet walker. He stamped. He whistled. He swung his mace at bushes that were in his way. Birds burst out of the trees ahead of him. Deer bounded off. A family of rabbits dived into their burrow and did not come out for an hour.

"Make way!" Bhima called cheerfully, to nobody in particular. "The strongest man in the world is coming through!"

A woodpecker stopped tapping to glare at him. A stream that had been chattering happily went quiet as he splashed across it. Bhima did not notice any of this. He was thinking about golden lotuses, and about the look on Draupadi's face when he came back with his arms full of them.

The higher he went, the colder the air became, and the more the mountain seemed to lean over him and watch. Mist curled between the pines. Somewhere far off, a waterfall hummed to itself. It was the kind of place that makes most people walk softly. Bhima walked exactly as loudly as before.

He climbed higher and higher. The trees grew shorter. The path grew narrower. And then, around a bend, the path grew blocked.

Lying right across it, from one side to the other, was a monkey.

He was a very old monkey. His fur was grey and thin, and his eyes were half closed, and his long, long tail lay stretched across the path like a fallen branch. He looked as though he had been asleep in that spot for about a hundred years.

"Excuse me," said Bhima loudly.

The monkey opened one eye.

"You are in my way," said Bhima. "Please move your tail so that I can pass."

"I am too old to move," said the monkey in a small, creaky voice. "My bones ache. My legs are tired. If you want to get by, young man, you will have to lift my tail yourself."

Bhima laughed. "Lift your tail? That is nothing. I have lifted elephants."

He bent down, took hold of the monkey's tail with one hand, and pulled.

The tail did not move.

Bhima frowned. He took hold of it with both hands and pulled harder. His face went red. His arms shook.

The tail did not move so much as a hair.

Bhima planted his feet. He put down his mace. He wrapped both arms around the old grey tail, took a deep breath, and heaved with all his enormous strength, the strength that had lifted elephants and knocked down walls.

The tail lay on the path, exactly where it was.

Bhima sat down, panting, and looked at the monkey. The monkey was watching him with both eyes open now, and there was a twinkle in them.

Slowly, Bhima felt something he did not feel very often. He felt small. And then he felt something else, something warmer. He felt curious.

"Who are you?" he asked, in a much quieter voice than before. "No ordinary monkey has a tail like that."

The old monkey sat up. And as he sat up, he seemed to grow a little, and his fur seemed to shine a little, and the twinkle in his eyes spread until his whole face was smiling.

"I am Hanuman," he said.

Bhima's mouth fell open. Every child in the land knew that name. Hanuman, who had leaped across the sea. Hanuman, who had carried a whole mountain in one hand. Hanuman, the greatest friend a prince ever had.

"But," said Bhima, "but you are my brother!"

For Bhima's father was the god of the wind, and so was Hanuman's. They had never met until this moment, on a narrow path, high in the mountains, with a tail between them.

Hanuman laughed, and it was the warmest laugh Bhima had ever heard. "Yes, little brother," he said. "I heard you coming from the bottom of the mountain. I heard you frightening the rabbits. And I thought, this one needs a lesson, but a gentle one."

Bhima bowed his head. "I was showing off," he said.

"Everyone does, sometimes," said Hanuman. "When I was young I once jumped at the sun because I thought it was a mango. So you see, I know a little about being big and foolish at the same time."

Bhima laughed in spite of himself, and the laugh seemed to loosen something in his chest.

"Tell me about the sea," he said suddenly. "They say you jumped across it."

So Hanuman told him. He told him about the shore where the monkeys sat feeling small, and the old bear who reminded him who he was, and the golden mountain that rose from the waves to offer him rest. He told it quietly, without boasting, the way you might describe a walk to the well.

"Were you afraid?" asked Bhima.

"Of course," said Hanuman. "Being brave is not the same as not being afraid. It is being afraid and going anyway, because someone you love is waiting."

Bhima thought about Draupadi by the stream, holding one golden flower to her face.

"I understand that part," he said.

"A little," said Hanuman kindly. "You are very strong, Bhima. But strength that stamps and shouts is only half of strength. The other half is knowing when to be quiet, and when to be gentle, and when to let someone else go first."

Bhima thought about the rabbits, and the deer, and the birds. He thought about how he had said, "make way," to a forest that had been there long before he was born.

"Will you show me?" he asked. "Your true self, I mean. Just once."

Hanuman stood. And he grew.

He grew until his head was above the pine trees. He grew until the clouds sat on his shoulders like a shawl. His fur shone like gold in the sun, and his eyes were as bright and kind as two moons. Bhima had to lie back on the path to see all of him, and even then he could not see the top.

Then, softly, Hanuman shrank back down, until he was just an old grey monkey again, sitting on a mountain path.

"That is how big I can be," he said. "And yet I choose to be small. Do you understand?"

"I think so," said Bhima.

Hanuman placed one hand on his brother's head. "Then here is my blessing. When you need to be strong, you will be stronger than anyone. And when you need to be gentle, you will remember this path."

He lifted his tail, curled it neatly around his feet, and pointed up the mountain. "Now. You were looking for golden lotuses, I think. There is a lake at the very top where they grow. Walk quietly, and you will find it."

Bhima picked up his mace. But this time he did not swing it at the bushes. He walked around them. He stepped over the rabbits' burrow instead of stamping past it. And when a deer looked up at him, he stood still and let it finish drinking.

At the top of the mountain he found the lake. It was round and blue and perfectly still, and floating on it were hundreds and hundreds of golden lotuses, each with a thousand petals, filling the whole mountaintop with their honey smell.

Bhima waded in, gently, so as not to stir the water. He gathered the flowers one by one, an armful and then another, until he could hardly see over the top of them.

The sun was setting when he came back down to the camp. Draupadi saw him coming, a great tall man hidden behind a mountain of golden flowers, and she laughed out loud with joy.

"A hundred," said Bhima, setting them down at her feet. "I said I would."

The other brothers came running. Arjuna picked up a lotus and turned it in the last light, counting petals until he lost count. Yudhishthira closed his eyes and breathed in the scent and smiled the slow smile he saved for the very best things. The twins, Nakula and Sahadeva, made a crown of flowers for Draupadi, and then one for each other, and then one for Bhima, who wore it without a word.

"But you are so quiet," said Draupadi. "What happened to you up there?"

"I met my brother," said Bhima. And he would not say anything more, because some things are too big to fit into words.

That night the whole camp smelled of golden lotuses. The brothers lay down on soft pine needles. The stream murmured. The stars came out one by one over the mountain.

And Bhima, the strongest man in the world, fell asleep as gently as a leaf settling on still water.
