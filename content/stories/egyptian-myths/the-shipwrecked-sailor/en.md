title: The Shipwrecked Sailor and the Golden Serpent
summary: Washed up alone on an island where everything grows, a sailor meets the largest and kindest creature he has ever seen.
moral: Even the biggest, strangest stranger may be a friend waiting to be found.

This is a story that a sailor told, long ago in Egypt, to a friend who was feeling small and frightened. It is a story about being frightened, and about what came after.

"Listen," said the sailor. "I will tell you about the time I was lost."

He had sailed on a great ship with a hundred and twenty men, the strongest and bravest in Egypt, down the long coast toward the mines of the south. The ship was as tall as a house and as long as a street, and the men sang as they rowed, and nobody was afraid of anything.

Then the wind changed.

It came out of a clear sky, sudden and enormous, and the sea rose up in a wave as high as a hill. The ship went up the wave and over the top and down the other side, and when it hit the bottom it broke like a clay pot.

The sailor remembered very little of what happened next. There was cold water. There was a plank of wood, which he held on to with both arms and would not let go. There was the sound of the storm, roaring, and then, slowly, fading.

And then there was sand.

He woke up face down on a beach, with the sun warm on his back and small waves tickling his feet. He was alive. He did not know how. He lay there for a long time just breathing, because breathing seemed like a very good thing to be able to do.

When at last he sat up, he saw that he was on an island.

It was the greenest island in the world. Fig trees grew right down to the water, heavy with fruit. Vines hung thick with grapes. Cucumbers and melons lay about on the ground as if someone had rolled them there. Birds sang in every tree, and fish swam in the shallows so slow and fat that he could have picked them up with his hands.

"Well," said the sailor to himself, "I shall not go hungry, at any rate."

He ate figs until his stomach hurt, and then, because he was a sailor and sailors are practical, he ate a few more for later. He drank from a stream of sweet water. He built a little fire from driftwood and cooked a fish, and it was the best fish he had ever eaten in his life, because he had been so sure he would never eat anything again.

Then, feeling grateful and a little less alone, he made a small offering of fruit to the gods on a flat stone, and said thank you.

That was when the ground began to shake.

The trees swayed. The birds went silent. The sailor stood very still with his heart thudding, and out of the forest, slowly, enormously, came a serpent.

It was longer than the ship had been. Its scales were pure gold and shone like the sun on water. Its eyebrows were made of blue lapis stone, and its beard, for it had a beard, was long and fine as silk. When it lifted its head, its head was higher than the tallest palm tree.

The sailor did what anyone would do. He fell flat on his face in the sand and did not move.

For a long moment there was silence. Then a voice spoke, and it was not at all the voice he expected. It was deep, yes, deep as a well. But it was gentle.

"Who has brought you here, little one? Who has brought you to my island?"

The sailor could not speak.

"If you do not answer," said the serpent, "I shall have to guess, and I am very bad at guessing. Come now. I will not hurt you."

Something in the voice made the sailor believe it. He lifted his head, just a little, and looked up into two golden eyes as big as shields. They were not angry. They were curious, and, he thought, a bit lonely.

So he told his story, with his voice shaking at first and then growing steadier. The great ship. The hundred and twenty men. The wave as high as a hill. The plank. The beach.

The serpent listened without moving. When the sailor finished, it lowered its enormous head until it rested on the sand quite close to him, so that they were nearly eye to eye.

"You are very brave to have told me all that," it said. "Now I will tell you something, so that we are even. Would you like to hear my story?"

"Yes," said the sailor, and found that he meant it.

"Once," said the serpent, "I was not alone on this island. There were seventy-five of us. My brothers and my sisters and my children, all golden, all shining. We lay in the sun together and talked, and the island rang with our voices."

"What happened to them?"

"A star fell," said the serpent quietly. "One night a star came down out of the sky, very bright, and when it touched the island there was a great light, and when the light faded, they had all gone up into the sky with it. I was away at the far end of the island, and so I stayed. And I have been here ever since, in the green and the quiet, with only the birds to talk to."

The sailor did not know what to say. So he did something braver than talking. He reached out and laid his hand, very lightly, on the serpent's golden nose.

"I am sorry," he said.

The serpent closed its great eyes for a moment.

"Do you know," it said, "in all the years since, no one has ever said that to me. Thank you, little sailor."

After that, they were friends.

The sailor stayed on the island for four months. He ate the figs and the fish, and he swam in the warm shallows, and every evening he sat beside the serpent's head as the sun went down and they talked. He told the serpent about Egypt, about the river and the markets and the songs the rowers sang. The serpent told him about the stars, which it had watched for a very long time and knew by name.

"Do you miss your home?" the serpent asked one evening.

"Every day," said the sailor. "I have a wife. I have children. They must think I am lost."

"You are not lost," said the serpent. "You are here. But I understand. Listen to me now, because I am old and I know things. In four months a ship will come. You will know the sailors on it. They will take you home, and you will hold your children, and you will grow old in your own house, and when your life is finished you will rest in your own land. This I promise you."

"How can you know that?"

"I have watched the sea a long time," said the serpent, and would say no more.

And in the fourth month, exactly as the serpent had said, a ship came. The sailor saw it from the top of a fig tree, a white sail far out on the blue, and he shouted until his voice cracked, and the sail turned toward him.

Before the boat reached the shore, the serpent came down to the beach for the last time.

"Take these," it said, and it laid gifts on the sand. Myrrh and sweet oils. Ivory. Bright feathers. Great lumps of incense that smelled like all the temples in Egypt. "Give them to your king, and tell him about my island. But do not try to find it again. When your ship has gone, this island will sink softly under the waves, and I will go up to join my family among the stars at last. It is time."

"I will never forget you," said the sailor.

"Nor I you," said the serpent. "You were the only one who said sorry."

The sailors on the ship were indeed men he knew. They wept to see him alive, and he wept to see them, and they loaded the gifts and turned the ship north toward home. When he looked back, the island was already only a green smudge on the sea, and then a shimmer, and then nothing at all, and high in the evening sky one new star had appeared, very bright and very golden.

Two months later he walked through his own door, and his children knocked him flat on the floor with the force of their hugging.

"So you see," the sailor said to his frightened friend, all those years later, "that is the story. I was more lost than anyone has ever been. And what I found, at the bottom of it all, was a friend."

The friend was quiet for a while. Then he said he felt a little better.

And if you ever look up on a clear night and see one star that is more golden than the rest, you might remember a green island under the sea, and a serpent with lapis eyebrows, and a sailor who reached out his hand, and slept easy on the sand.
