## The Gift of Wheat

Long ago, before there were fields in Egypt, there was only the river and the sand and the people, who did not yet know what to do with either.

They gathered what they could. They fished a little. In the hungry months they were hungry, and they did not understand why.

Then Osiris became king.

He was the eldest son of the sky and the earth, tall and calm, with skin the green of new leaves and a gentle way of speaking that made people lean in to listen. His queen was his sister Isis, who was the cleverest of all the gods, and the kindest, and who could not pass a crying child without stopping.

The two of them looked at the hungry people and decided that this would not do.

Osiris walked out to the muddy bank after the river had flooded and gone down. He knelt and pressed a handful of small hard seeds into the wet earth. "Wait," he said to the people watching. They waited. And when the green shoots came up, and grew tall, and turned to gold, Osiris showed them how to cut the stalks and beat out the grain and grind it and bake it, and that was the first bread in Egypt.

Isis showed the women how to spin flax into thread and weave it into linen, so that everyone had clothes that were cool in the heat. She taught them which plants healed a fever and which soothed a burn. She taught them songs to sing while they worked, so that the work went faster.

The hungry months ended. Villages grew along the river. Children grew fat and loud. And in every house, when the family sat down to eat, somebody would say, "Thank Osiris for the bread. Thank Isis for the cloth." And they meant it.

Osiris was so happy with Egypt that he wanted to share it. He left Isis to rule and set off to travel the whole world, teaching people everywhere how to plant and harvest. He was gone for years, and wherever he went, fields sprang up behind him.

He went north to lands where the winters were cold and taught people to store grain against the snow. He went east to lands of mountains and showed them how to cut steps into the hillsides so that the fields would hold. He went south along the river to its very source and sat with the people there under a great tree and listened to their songs before he taught them anything, because Osiris always listened first.

Everywhere he went he left the same three gifts: seeds, and the knowing of how to plant them, and the habit of saying thank you at supper.

But not everybody was happy.

Osiris and Isis had a brother named Set. Set was red-haired and restless and quick to anger, and he lived at the edge of the desert, where nothing grew. He was not a monster. He was a person, or a god, who had gotten stuck in a feeling and could not find his way out of it.

The feeling was this: everybody loved Osiris.

Set watched the people thank his brother for bread. He watched them sing songs about his brother's kindness. He watched Isis rule the country so well that nobody even noticed the king was away. And the feeling grew in him like a thorn bush, prickly and tangled, until he could not see past it.

"Why him?" Set muttered to the desert. "Why always him?"

The desert, being a desert, did not answer. But Set began to make a plan.

## The Beautiful Chest

When Osiris came home from his long journey, the whole of Egypt threw a feast.

There were lamps along the river as far as anyone could see. There was music and roasted goose and honey cakes. Isis stood at Osiris's side in a linen dress so fine that it looked like mist, and she could not stop smiling.

Set came too. He brought a gift.

It was a chest, long and beautiful, made of cedar wood and covered in gold and blue stone, with lotus flowers carved along the sides. Everybody gasped when they saw it.

"I had it made specially," said Set, smiling a wide smile that did not quite reach his eyes. "But I could not decide who should have it. So here is a game. Whoever fits inside it exactly, lying down, shall keep it."

One by one the guests tried. The chest was too long for most of them, or too short, or too wide. They laughed and climbed out again.

Then Osiris tried.

He lay down inside, and it fitted him perfectly, because Set had measured him secretly while he slept, weeks before.

"It fits!" said Osiris, laughing. "Set, it is wonderful. Thank you."

"Yes," said Set. "It is exactly right."

And he closed the lid.

Before anyone understood what was happening, Set's friends had fastened the lid with straps and lifted the chest onto their shoulders and carried it out through the crowd and down to the river, and pushed it into the current, and the dark water took it and swept it away northward, toward the sea.

The music stopped. The lamps seemed to dim.

Isis stood frozen for one heartbeat. Then she ran to the bank, but the chest was already a speck in the moonlight, and then it was gone.

She did not scream. She did not fall down. She was Isis, and she was the cleverest of the gods, and she understood at once that screaming would not bring him back. Searching might.

She cut off a lock of her hair, which is what people did in those days when their hearts were broken. She put on a plain dress. And she walked down to the river and began to follow it.

She followed it for a very long time.

She asked the fishermen. "Have you seen a golden chest go by?" Some had. Some had not. She followed the ones who had.

She asked the children playing at the water's edge, because children see everything, and one of them pointed north and said, "That way, lady. It was shining." She thanked him and gave him a fig and walked on.

She asked the birds. Birds go everywhere and forget nothing, and a heron told her the chest had floated all the way to the sea and along the coast to a far country called Byblos, where it had come to rest on a beach.

So Isis went to Byblos.

On the beach she found no chest. But she found a tree.

It was a tamarisk tree, young and enormous, standing right at the water's edge, and it was the most beautiful tree anyone had ever seen. It had grown up around the chest, wrapping it in its trunk, hiding it completely inside living wood, as if it had known that something precious needed protecting.

The king of Byblos had seen the wonderful tree and had it cut down and carried to his palace to be a pillar in his great hall. Isis went to the palace. She did not say who she was. She sat by a well and combed the hair of the queen's maids and made them laugh, and when the queen heard about the kind stranger with the sad eyes, she sent for her and made her the nurse of the little prince.

Isis did not push through the palace gates demanding her husband. She was cleverer than that, and sadder, and she knew that a queen who bursts in shouting gets thrown out, while a kind stranger who is patient gets asked to stay.

So she sat by the well in the courtyard where the queen's maids came for water, and when they came she offered to braid their hair, and her fingers were so gentle and her braids so beautiful that they came back the next day, and the next, and each time they went away smelling faintly of lotus, which was the scent of Isis herself.

Isis loved the little prince. She rocked him to sleep every night beside the pillar, and she sang to him, and only the pillar knew that she was really singing to the one inside it.

At last she told the queen the truth. The queen wept, and the king had the pillar carefully cut open, and there was the golden chest, quite safe, smelling of cedar.

Isis put her hand on the lid.

"I found you," she whispered. "I told you I would."

## Fourteen Hiding Places

Isis brought the chest home to Egypt in a boat.

She did not open it on the journey. She was afraid, a little, of what she would see. But she sat beside it every day with her hand on the lid, and she talked to Osiris about the river, about the fields, about how the wheat was coming along without him, and the boat moved slowly south against the current.

When she reached the marshes of the delta, where the river spreads into a hundred green channels full of reeds and herons and frogs, she hid the chest among the papyrus and went to find her sister Nephthys, who was quiet and thoughtful and knew every path through the marsh.

But Set had been watching for her.

He had been watching the river for months, red-haired and restless, sure that his clever sister would come back. And on the night she left the chest in the reeds, he found it, and he did something that even he did not fully understand. He could not bear to see Osiris found. He could not bear to see him brought home. So he took the chest, and he carried it deep into the marshes, and he hid it, and then he hid it again, in fourteen different places, moving it from island to island in the dark, so that even he could not remember, afterward, quite where it was.

Then he went back to his desert and sat down and did not feel any better at all.

When Isis returned with Nephthys and found the reeds empty, she sat down on the wet ground and, for the first time since the feast, she cried.

Nephthys sat with her. She did not say anything. She held her sister's hand, and the frogs sang in the dark, and after a while Isis wiped her face and said, "Fourteen places. Very well. Then we will look in fourteen places."

They made a boat of papyrus, light and quick. Nephthys steered. Isis sat at the front and watched. And the marsh, which had known Isis since she first taught the fishermen to weave nets, began to help.

The frogs told them which channels were deep. The herons stood on one leg on the islands and turned their heads toward hiding places. A crocodile, which is not an animal that helps very often, drifted alongside them one whole afternoon like a log, and where it stopped, they found the first of Set's markers.

Once, when the boat stuck fast on a mudbank and neither sister could push it free, a hippopotamus rose out of the water beside them, huge and grey and dripping, and looked at them for a long moment with small wise eyes. Then it put its enormous head against the stern and shoved, and the boat slid off the mud like a leaf, and the hippopotamus sank back under the water without a word.

"Thank you," said Isis to the ripples.

And they were joined by someone else.

Out of the reeds one evening came a young god with the head of a jackal, sleek and black, carrying a small lantern. This was Anubis, and he was very young then, and shy, and he had heard about the search and wanted to help.

"I am good at finding things," he said. "I can smell where people have been. And I do not mind the dark."

"Then come," said Isis, and she made room for him in the boat.

Anubis put his nose down to the water and the reeds and the mud, and he found the trail. Island after island, hiding place after hiding place, he led them, and each time they found where the chest had rested, and each time it had moved on. But each time they were closer.

It took them many days. They slept in the boat. They ate what the marsh gave them. Isis grew thin and quiet and did not stop.

On the fourteenth day, at sunset, on a tiny island so far into the marsh that no one had ever named it, they found the golden chest half-sunk in soft mud with reeds growing over it.

Anubis sat down beside it and did not know what to do with his face, so he simply looked pleased.

Isis knelt in the mud and put both hands on the lid.

"Fourteen places," she said softly. "I found you fourteen times."

## The King of the Evening Land

They opened the chest together, on the little island, as the last of the sun went down.

Osiris lay inside as if he were only asleep. His green skin was pale. His eyes were closed. He had been in the dark for a very long time.

Isis had one more thing to try.

She sent Anubis with his lantern across the marsh to find Thoth, the wise ibis-headed god, who knew every word of power there is. Thoth came at once, splashing through the shallows with his long legs, carrying a scroll. He knelt beside the chest and read the words, slowly, one after another, and Isis spread her arms over Osiris and, because she was a goddess and could do such things, she became a bird, a kite with wide brown wings, and beat the air over him so that the words could breathe.

Osiris opened his eyes.

He looked at Isis. He looked at Nephthys, and at Thoth, and at the young jackal god who had found him. He smiled his slow, gentle smile.

"You came," he said. "All this way."

"All this way," said Isis, becoming herself again, and she put her head down on his chest and stayed there for a long time.

But something had changed. Osiris had been to the edge of the evening land, the quiet country where people go when their long lives are done, and he had seen that it had no king. Nobody was there to welcome the tired travellers when they arrived. Nobody kept the fields green or the light soft.

"I cannot stay here," he said gently. "Not the way I was. I have seen the evening land, and it needs someone. I think it needs me."

Isis had known, somewhere inside, that he would say this. She had found him. That had always been the point. Keeping him was something else.

"Then go and be its king," she said. "Make it kind. And I will stay here and rule Egypt, and raise our son, and when the people of Egypt have finished their lives and come to you, you will know them by their bread, and you will say, 'Isis taught your grandmother to weave.'"

Osiris laughed for the first time since the feast.

So he went. He became the king of the Field of Reeds, the evening land, and he made it exactly as he had promised. Green fields. Slow water. Light like a lamp through linen. And a tall calm figure at the gate who says to every tired traveller, "Come in. You must be hungry. There is bread."

Isis went home. She had a baby, a boy called Horus with a falcon's bright eyes, and she hid him in the marshes and raised him among the frogs and herons until he was strong enough to be king. He grew up to be a good one.

Horus grew up wild and happy in the marsh. He learned to swim before he could walk. He learned the names of every bird from the herons who had helped find his father. When he was frightened at night, his mother told him stories about a tall green king who had taught the world to make bread, and Horus would fall asleep saying, "Tell me again about the wheat." He never met his father face to face. But he knew him by heart, which is a different way of knowing, and just as good.

And Set? Set was called before all the gods to explain himself. He stood there red-haired and sullen, and when they asked him why, he found that he could not say. "Everybody loved him," he muttered at last. "And nobody..." and he stopped.

Isis looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, "You may not live by the river any more. Go to the desert. Be the god of storms and sand and the wild edges of things. It is honest work, and it suits you, and nobody will make you watch them love someone else."

Set went. He was better there. The desert needed a god too, and some say he even came to like it, roaring about in the dust storms with nobody to compare himself to.

As for the river, every year since then, at the same season, it rises and floods the fields and lays down rich black mud, and the wheat grows tall and gold. The old grandmothers say the flood is Isis's tears, and that she is not crying because she is sad. She is crying because she found him, fourteen times, and because he is safe, and because every evening, when the sun sets red over the marsh, she knows exactly where he is.

Sleep now. Somewhere in the evening land, the fields are green, the water is slow, and the light is like a lamp through linen. A tall, calm king is standing at the gate.

There is always bread.
