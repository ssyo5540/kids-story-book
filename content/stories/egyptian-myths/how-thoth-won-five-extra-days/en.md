moral: A clever, kind mind can find a way through almost anything.

At the very beginning of things, in the land of Egypt, the sky was a goddess and the earth was a god.

The sky goddess was called Nut. She was tall and dark and covered all over in stars, and she arched over the world on the tips of her fingers and toes. The earth god was called Geb. He lay beneath her, green and brown and patient, with rivers for veins and mountains for knees.

Nut and Geb loved each other very much. And because they loved each other, they wanted children.

But Ra, the sun god, who was king over everything, did not like this idea at all. Ra was rather old and rather set in his ways, and he was afraid that the children of the sky and the earth would grow up to be great gods themselves, and that one of them might, one day, want to be king instead of him.

So Ra made a rule.

"Nut may not have a child," he declared, "on any day of the year."

Now in those days the year had exactly three hundred and sixty days, and Ra knew every one of them by name. He had made his rule very carefully. Not one day was left out.

Nut wept. Her tears fell as gentle rain, and Geb felt them on his face and wept too, and for a while the whole world was rather damp.

But Nut had a friend.

His name was Thoth. He had the head of an ibis, a long-legged river bird with a curved beak, and the sharpest mind in all of Egypt. Thoth had invented writing. He had invented counting. He kept the records of the gods and knew the answer to nearly every question. And when he could not find an answer, he sat very still and thought until one arrived.

Thoth heard Nut crying, and he came and sat beside her on the edge of the sky.

"Ra has forbidden me a child on any day of the year," she said. "And there are no other days."

Thoth tipped his long head to one side.

"Not yet," he said.

He went home to his house of scrolls, where every day of the year was written down in a long neat column, and he read the column from top to bottom three times. Three hundred and sixty days. Not one missing. Not one spare. Ra had been thorough, as Ra always was.

"A year is a year," Thoth said to himself. "You cannot add to it. Unless..." And he sat down at his table and stared at nothing for a very long time, which is what thinking looks like from the outside.

He thought about it all afternoon. He thought about it into the evening, while the stars came out on Nut's shoulders. And when the moon rose, round and white and rather pleased with itself, Thoth suddenly smiled.

The moon in those days was a god called Khonsu, and Khonsu had two great loves. He loved his own light, which he was very proud of, and he loved playing games. Most of all he loved senet, a board game of little squares and little pieces, which the gods played on long quiet nights.

Thoth picked up his senet board and flew up to the moon.

"Good evening, Khonsu," he said. "You look very bright tonight."

"I always look very bright," said the moon.

"Would you care for a game?"

Khonsu's eyes lit up. Nobody ever wanted to play with him, because he sulked when he lost. "What shall we play for?" he asked, because a game is no fun without something to win.

Thoth pretended to think. "Oh," he said, "something small. If you win, I will write a poem about how bright you are, and every scribe in Egypt will copy it. And if I win, you will give me a little of your light. Just a sliver. You would hardly notice."

"A sliver of light? Ha!" said Khonsu, who was very sure he would not lose. "Agreed."

They set up the board. Thoth moved his pieces slowly and carefully. Khonsu moved his quickly and grandly. And Thoth won.

Senet is a game of thirty squares and little carved pieces, and you throw sticks to see how far you may move. Khonsu threw the sticks high and caught them with a flourish. Thoth threw them low and studied where they fell. Khonsu rushed his pieces toward the end of the board. Thoth waited, and blocked, and waited again, and when Khonsu was sure he had won, Thoth slipped one last piece past him and off the board, and that was that.

"Best of three," said Khonsu.

Thoth won again.

"Best of five," said the moon, a little pink.

Thoth won that one too.

They played all night long, game after game, while the stars wheeled overhead. Sometimes Khonsu won, and crowed about it, and Thoth congratulated him warmly. But mostly Thoth won, quietly and politely, one small sliver of moonlight at a time.

By the time the sky began to turn grey, Thoth had a whole jar of moonlight sitting beside him, glowing softly, and the moon looked just a little thinner than it had the night before.

Down below, Nut had not slept. She had lain all night along the top of the world with her stars burning, listening to the faint click of senet pieces far above her, and beside her Geb had held very still so as not to shake the board.

"Is it working?" he whispered.

"I do not know," she whispered back. "But the moon is getting thinner. That must mean something."

"That is enough for tonight, I think," said Thoth, tucking the board under his wing. "Thank you, Khonsu. You play very well."

"I do, don't I?" said the moon, and he went off to bed feeling rather good about the whole thing.

Thoth flew back down to Nut with his jar of moonlight. He counted it carefully, because counting was his special gift. He had won exactly one seventy-second part of the moon's light. And when he spread it out, thin and bright, and stitched it onto the end of the year, it made five whole new days.

Five days that had never existed before. Five days that Ra had never named. Five days that were not part of the year at all.

"There," said Thoth to Nut. "Ra said you may not have a child on any day of the year. He said nothing about these."

Nut laughed for the first time in months, and her laughter was the sound of wind in the reeds.

On the first of the five new days, her first child was born. He was called Osiris, and he was gentle and good, and one day he would teach people how to grow wheat and barley.

On the second day came Horus the Elder, with the wings of a falcon.

On the third day came Set, who was loud and stormy and red-haired and always in a hurry.

On the fourth day came Isis, who was clever like Thoth and kind like her mother, and who would grow up to be the wisest of them all.

And on the fifth day came Nephthys, quiet and thoughtful, who liked the evening best.

Five babies in five days is a great many babies, even for a goddess. Nut lay along the top of the sky with all of them tucked into the curve of her arms, and Geb rumbled lullabies from below, and Thoth sat nearby writing down their names very carefully in a brand-new scroll, because he knew they would be important one day.

Ra found out, of course. He came storming across the sky with his face blazing, ready to be very angry with somebody. But when he looked down and saw five small new gods tumbling about on the grass, giggling and grabbing each other's toes, something in his old heart went soft.

"Well," he grumbled, "I suppose I did not say anything about those days."

"You did not," agreed Thoth, very respectfully.

"Hmph," said Ra. And he went on his way across the sky, and he never made another rule about days again.

As for the moon, he never quite got his sliver of light back. That is why, the old storytellers say, the moon is not always full. Once a month he shines as round and bright as he did the night before the game. Then, night by night, he grows a little thinner, remembering all the senet he lost to a clever bird, until he is only a curl of silver, and then he starts again.

He does not really mind. It gives him something to think about on the long nights.

And every year, at the very end, the five extra days still come round, the days that belong to nobody's rule, the days that were made out of moonlight by a friend who sat very still and thought until an answer arrived.

Look out of the window tonight. If the moon is thin, you know why.

He has been playing senet with Thoth again.
