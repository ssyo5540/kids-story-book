moral: The night is just the sun on its way back to you.

In the land of Egypt, where the river is wide and the sand is gold, the people used to say that the sun was a boat.

Every morning, at the very edge of the world, a great golden boat rose up out of the eastern hills. And standing at the front of it, with the head of a falcon and a round shining disc on his brow, stood Ra, the god of the sun.

The boat was called the Boat of a Million Years. It had no sail and no oars. It moved because Ra wished it to move. And as it rose, the whole sky turned pink, and then gold, and then blue, and every rooster in Egypt threw back its head and crowed.

"Good morning," said Ra to the world.

His daughter Ma'at stood beside him, holding a white feather, to make sure the boat always sailed straight and fair. Thoth, the wise ibis-headed god, sat at the back with a scroll, writing down everything that happened. And a crew of small bright gods pulled on ropes of light.

Slowly, slowly, the golden boat climbed the sky.

Down below, Egypt woke up. Farmers walked out to their fields. Children ran to the river to fill jugs. Fishermen pushed their boats into the water, and the river flashed and sparkled, because Ra was smiling at it.

At noon the boat reached the very top of the sky, and Ra was so bright that nobody could look straight at him. Cats lay flat in the shade. Lizards baked on the walls. Even the river seemed to slow down and yawn.

Then, just as slowly, the boat began to sail down the other side.

The afternoon grew long and soft. Shadows stretched out across the sand. The sky turned orange, then rose, then a deep, deep purple, and the golden boat came down, down, down toward the western hills.

"Good night," said Ra to the world.

And the boat slipped over the edge of the sky and was gone.

But this was not the end of Ra's day. Oh, no. This was the most important part.

Because under the world, the people said, there was another river, a dark one, that ran all the way from the west back to the east. And the only way for the sun to get back to where it started was to sail through the night.

So at the edge of the world, Ra stepped from his golden day boat into his silver night boat. The crew lit lamps along its sides. Ma'at held her feather high. Thoth unrolled a fresh scroll. And the night boat slid into the dark river under the earth.

It was very quiet down there. The water was black and still. On either bank, the spirits of people who had lived long ago waved to Ra as he passed, and he waved back, and for a little while his light warmed them, and they were glad.

Now, in the middle of the dark river, there lived a serpent named Apep.

Apep was enormous. He was as long as the whole river, and his scales were the colour of shadows, and he did not like the light one bit. Every single night, as the silver boat came gliding along, Apep would rise up out of the water and try to swallow the sun.

He never managed it.

The way the old grandmothers liked to tell it, it was the singing that stopped him.

For the crew of the night boat knew a song, a slow and gentle song, so soft and so sleepy that even a serpent as long as a river could not stay awake to hear the end of it. They sang it as they passed, and Apep's great eyes grew heavy, and his great head sank down onto the bank, and he was snoring before the boat had gone by.

"Good night, Apep," said Ra, quite kindly, because even a sun god knows that a sleepy serpent is not really a bad serpent.

On the boat sailed, hour after hour, through the long dark. The lamps glowed. The river whispered. The crew hummed the sleepy song to themselves.

And at last, far ahead, the darkness grew a little grey. Then a little pink. The river bent upward. The silver boat rose, and rose, and Ra stepped back into his golden boat, and the eastern hills opened like a door.

Somewhere in Egypt a rooster crowed.

"Good morning," said Ra to the world.

So you see, when the sun goes down tonight, it is not gone. It is only sailing, down the quiet river under the world, singing serpents to sleep, on its way back to you.

Close your eyes. The boat is already on its way.
