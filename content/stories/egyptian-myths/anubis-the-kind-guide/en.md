title: Anubis, the Kind Guide
summary: A jackal-headed god with a lantern and a gentle voice walks beside an old fisherman on the quietest journey of all, and shows him that a kind heart is lighter than a feather.
moral: A heart full of kindness is the lightest thing there is.

---

In the land of Egypt, long ago, people used to say that when someone had finished their long life, they did not simply stop. They went on a journey. And the journey had a guide.

The guide's name was Anubis.

He had the body of a man and the head of a jackal, sleek and black, with tall pointed ears that could hear a whisper from across a desert. Some people thought he must be frightening, because of the jackal head. But those people had never met him. Anubis had the softest voice of all the gods, and he carried a small lantern, and he was never, ever in a hurry.

This is the story of one of the people he walked with.

His name was Nebi, and he was a fisherman. For eighty years he had lived in a mud-brick house on the bank of the great river Nile, with a small wooden boat, a small brown dog, and a very large family. He had caught fish every morning of his life. He had mended nets in the afternoon. In the evenings he had sat on his doorstep and told stories to his grandchildren, and then his great-grandchildren, until they fell asleep in heaps around his feet.

It had been a good life. A long, full, ordinary, wonderful life.

And when it was finished, Nebi found himself standing at the edge of the river in the very early morning, before the sun was up, with the mist lying on the water. He felt light. He felt rested. He felt about forty years younger. And he was not at all sure where he was supposed to go.

That was when he noticed the lantern.

It was coming toward him along the bank, a small warm glow in the grey, and holding it was a tall figure with a jackal's head.

Nebi had heard stories about Anubis all his life. He stood up a little straighter. He was not exactly afraid. But he was not exactly not afraid either.

Anubis stopped a few steps away and lowered his lantern so that its light fell gently on the fisherman's face.

"Good morning, Nebi," he said. His voice was like the river at night, low and steady. "I have come to walk with you, if you would like company."

"I would like that very much," said Nebi, who found to his surprise that he meant it.

"Then let us go. It is not far. And there is no rush at all."

They walked west, along the river, away from the coming sun. Nebi's small brown dog was not there, and he missed him, and he said so.

"Dogs know the way better than anyone," said Anubis. "You will see him before long."

As they walked, the river began to change. The water grew stiller and brighter. The reeds along the bank grew taller and greener than any Nebi had ever seen. Lotuses opened as they passed, and the mist lifted, and the sky turned the soft gold of a lamp seen through linen.

"I fished this river for eighty years," said Nebi, "and I never saw it like this."

"You were busy," said Anubis kindly. "You had nets to mend."

They talked, the way travellers do. Nebi told him about his wife, who had gone ahead on this same journey some years before. He told him about his boat, which had a crack in it that he had patched thirty times. He told him about the time his youngest granddaughter had fallen in the river and he had jumped in after her with all his clothes on, and how they had both come out laughing and covered in weed.

Anubis listened to every word. He was very good at listening. It was, in fact, the thing he was best at.

At last they came to a great hall, open to the sky, with tall pillars painted every colour there is. In the middle of the hall stood a set of golden scales.

Nebi stopped in the doorway.

"Ah," he said. "I have heard about this part."

"Yes," said Anubis. "This is where we find out how heavy your heart is. But there is nothing to be nervous about. Come and see."

Beside the scales stood a goddess with a single white feather in her hair. Her name was Ma'at, and she was the spirit of everything true and fair. She smiled at Nebi and took the feather from her hair and laid it on one side of the scales, where it rested, light as breath.

"Now your heart," she said. "Not the one in your chest. The other one. The one made of everything you have done."

Nebi did not know how to give her that. But Anubis touched his shoulder, and Nebi found that he was holding something in his two hands, something warm and glowing, about the size of a pomegranate. He looked at it. He could see, inside it, little flickers of pictures. His wife's face. A fish leaping. A small girl laughing in the water. His doorstep at dusk with children asleep around it.

He set it, very carefully, on the other side of the scales.

For a moment nothing moved. Then, slowly, the scales settled, and the two sides hung level. The heart, full of eighty years, weighed exactly as much as one white feather.

Nebi let out a breath he had not known he was holding.

"Kindness is very light," said Anubis softly. "People are always surprised. They think a long life must be heavy. But every kind thing you did made your heart a little lighter, until it could float."

"I was not always kind," said Nebi honestly. "I shouted at my nets. I was grumpy before breakfast."

"Everybody is grumpy before breakfast," said Ma'at, and she laughed, and her laugh was like small bells. "Go on, Nebi. You have passed. Not because you were perfect. Because you loved, and because you tried."

Anubis lifted his lantern again and led Nebi out through the far door of the hall.

Beyond it lay a field.

It stretched as far as Nebi could see, green and gold, with a river winding through it that looked very much like the Nile, only cleaner and slower. Reeds swayed. Birds sang. The light was the light of a perfect evening, the kind that comes just before supper, and it never seemed to change.

Down by the water, pulled up on the bank, was a small wooden boat with a crack in it that had been patched thirty times.

And sitting beside the boat, thumping his tail on the ground so hard that the dust flew up, was a small brown dog.

Nebi made a sound that was half a laugh and half something else, and he began to walk very fast, and then to run, and the dog ran to meet him, and they fell over together in the grass.

Anubis stood at the edge of the field and watched, and if a jackal can smile, he smiled.

Farther along the bank, a woman was walking toward them with a basket of bread, and Nebi knew her walk before he could see her face.

"Thank you," he called back over his shoulder. "Thank you for walking with me."

"It was my pleasure," said Anubis. "It always is."

He turned back toward the hall, and the mist, and the river, where someone else, somewhere, was standing on a bank in the early morning, not quite sure where to go. There was always someone. That was the whole of his work, and he had never wanted any other.

Behind him, in the Field of Reeds, an old fisherman sat down on his doorstep with a dog across his feet, and the evening light stayed exactly where it was, and it was time for stories.

If you ever hear that Anubis is frightening, remember Nebi. Remember the lantern, and the slow walk by the river, and the heart that weighed the same as a feather.

Then close your eyes, and let your own heart grow a little lighter, and sleep.
