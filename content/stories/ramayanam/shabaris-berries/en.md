---
title: Shabari's Berries
summary: An old woman keeps a little hut in the forest ready for a guest she has waited for all her life, and learns that love makes even the simplest gift perfect.
moral: A gift given with love is always enough.

Deep in a green forest, far from any town, there was a small round hut with a roof made of leaves. In front of the hut was a path of flat stones, and along the path grew bushes heavy with dark red berries.

An old woman lived in the hut. Her name was Shabari. Her hair was white as milk and her back was bent like a bow, but her eyes were bright, and her hands were never still.

Every morning, before the sun was fully awake, Shabari swept the path of flat stones. She swept it slowly and carefully, so that not one thorn and not one sharp pebble was left behind.

"Someone is coming," she would say to herself, "and his feet must not hurt."

Then she would fill a clay pot with cool water from the stream and set it by the door. She would pick the sweetest berries from the bushes and lay them on a large green leaf. And she would sit in the doorway and wait.

The animals of the forest knew her well. A pair of deer came to drink from the stream each morning. A family of monkeys chattered in the tree above her hut. A slow old tortoise crossed her path every afternoon and always took exactly the same route.

Shabari smiled at all of them. "Not yet," she would tell them. "But soon."

Long ago, when Shabari was young, she had come to live in this forest with a wise old teacher. He had taught her to be kind to every creature, to speak softly, and to keep her heart open like a door.

Before the teacher went to rest among the stars, he had told her one more thing. "One day," he said, "a prince named Rama will pass this way. He will be tired and far from home. Welcome him, Shabari. Your kindness will matter more than you can know."

So Shabari had waited. Spring after spring, the berry bushes flowered and fruited. Rain after rain, she mended the leaf roof of her hut. Her hair turned from black to grey to white.

And every single morning, she swept the path.

In the summer, when the air was hot and still, she carried water to the young trees along the path so that they would grow tall and give shade to her guest. In the rainy season, she laid flat stones across the muddy places so that his feet would stay dry.

In the cool season, when the mornings were misty, she gathered dry leaves and kept a small fire ready by the door. "He may be cold when he comes," she said to the deer. "A traveller is always a little cold."

Sometimes other travellers did pass by. A woodcutter with a bundle on his head. A girl looking for a lost goat. A merchant who had taken a wrong turn. Shabari welcomed every one of them. She gave them water and berries and pointed them toward the right road.

"Are you the one I am waiting for?" she would ask each of them, just in case.

They would shake their heads, puzzled, and thank her, and go on their way. And Shabari would smile and wave and go back to her sweeping. Every kind welcome, she thought, was good practice for the one that mattered most.

Some days a doubt would tap at her, the way a woodpecker taps at a tree. "Maybe he has forgotten," the doubt would say. "Maybe he took another road."

But Shabari would only shake her head and pick up her broom. "My teacher said he would come," she said. "So I will be ready."

Now, one afternoon, the forest grew very still. The monkeys stopped chattering. The deer lifted their heads. Even the old tortoise paused in the middle of the path, which he had never done before.

Two young men were walking between the trees. Their clothes were made of bark and leaves, and their faces were tired and dusty. But they walked gently, careful not to step on a single flower.

The taller one had a bow across his back and a face as calm as a lake at dawn. The other walked just behind him, looking left and right, watching over his brother like a shadow that cares.

Shabari stood up so quickly that her broom fell over.

"Rama," she whispered. She did not need anyone to tell her. She knew him the way you know the sound of rain before you see it.

The prince stopped at the edge of the path of flat stones. He looked at the clean stones, and the clay pot of water, and the berries on the green leaf. Then he looked at the old woman in the doorway, and he pressed his palms together and bowed his head.

"Amma," he said. "May we rest here a while? We have been walking for a very long time."

Shabari could hardly speak. She hurried forward and took his hands in her small wrinkled ones. "Come, come," she said. "Sit. Sit. I have been waiting for you."

"For me?" asked Rama, surprised.

"For years and years," said Shabari, and she laughed, and a tear rolled down her cheek at the same time.

Lakshmana, the younger brother, looked around the little clearing. He saw how every stone had been swept and how the water pot was full and cool. He understood, and he bowed to her as well.

Shabari brought them water first. Rama drank slowly, and some of the tiredness left his face, the way dust leaves a leaf when the rain touches it.

Then she brought the green leaf of berries and set it before them. But she did not sit back. She picked up one berry, looked at it closely, and bit into it herself.

Lakshmana's eyebrows went up. In palaces, no one ever tasted the food before offering it to a guest. It was not the proper thing to do.

Shabari did not notice. "This one is sweet," she said, and she placed it in Rama's hand. She picked up another and tasted it. "This one is a little sour," she said, and she set it aside. "Not for you."

Berry after berry she tasted, choosing only the sweetest ones and placing them gently in Rama's palm.

Lakshmana opened his mouth to say something. But Rama caught his eye and smiled, a small quiet smile that said, wait.

Rama ate the berries one at a time. He closed his eyes as he ate, as though each one were the most delicious thing in the world.

"Amma," he said when the leaf was empty, "I have eaten in great halls. I have eaten fruit from royal gardens. But I have never tasted anything as sweet as these berries."

"They are only forest berries," said Shabari, looking down.

"No," said Rama. "They are berries you tasted first, so that I would never bite into a sour one. Every berry was chosen with love. That is why they are the sweetest."

Lakshmana looked at the old woman, and at his brother, and he felt something warm settle in his chest. He had learned a lesson too, without a single word being spoken to him.

"Amma," Rama asked gently, "who told you that I would come this way?"

Shabari's eyes grew soft. "My teacher," she said. "A long time ago, before he went to rest among the stars. He said, wait for Rama, and welcome him, and your kindness will matter."

"Your teacher was wise," said Rama. "And you were patient. That is a rare pair of gifts."

"Patience is easy," said Shabari, "when you know that what you wait for is good."

The afternoon turned golden. Shabari told them about the forest, about the deer and the monkeys and the slow old tortoise. Rama listened to every word as if it were a story from a great book.

Then Rama asked her a question. "Amma, we are looking for someone very dear to us. Do you know which way we should go?"

Shabari nodded. She had lived in this forest for a lifetime, and she knew every path and every stream. She pointed toward the west, where the sun was slipping lower. "Beyond that hill there is a lake," she said. "The water is clear as glass. Rest there, and then walk on. You will find friends who can help you."

Rama thanked her. Then he did something that made the whole forest seem to hold its breath. He bowed down and touched the old woman's feet, the way a child bows before a mother.

"You waited for me," he said softly. "Not with gold or silk. With a swept path and cool water and sweet berries. That is the truest welcome I have ever received."

Shabari put her hand on his head. She did not have any words left. She did not need any.

When the two brothers walked on toward the western hill, the animals of the forest walked a little way with them. The deer led the way. The monkeys swung from branch to branch above. The tortoise, of course, was left far behind, but he seemed pleased anyway.

Shabari stood in her doorway and watched until the two figures were tiny, and then until they were gone.

She felt very light, the way you feel when you have finished a long, long task. Her back did not ache. Her hands were still at last.

She sat down in the doorway, and the evening breeze moved through the berry bushes, and the leaves whispered like an old friend humming a tune.

"He came," she said to the deer, who had returned to drink from the stream. "He came, and I was ready."

The stars came out slowly, one by one, over the little round hut. The monkeys curled up in the tree. The tortoise found his favourite stone and tucked in his head. The water in the clay pot lay perfectly still.

And Shabari closed her bright old eyes, with a smile on her face, and slept the deep and peaceful sleep of someone who has waited well, and loved well, and has nothing left to wait for.

The forest was quiet. The path was clean. And all was well.
