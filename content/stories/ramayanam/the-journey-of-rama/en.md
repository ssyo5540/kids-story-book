## The Prince of Ayodhya

Long ago, on the banks of a wide bright river, there was a city called Ayodhya.

It had white walls and golden gates, and gardens where peacocks walked, and every evening the sound of bells came drifting over the rooftops from a hundred temples. The people of Ayodhya were happy, because their king was good. His name was Dasharatha, and he had ruled for a very long time, and he had only one sorrow. He had no children.

He prayed, and he waited, and at last, in one wonderful year, four sons were born to his three queens.

The eldest was Rama, son of Queen Kausalya. He had dark skin the colour of a rain cloud and eyes that were calm even when he was a baby. Then came Bharata, son of Queen Kaikeyi, who was quick and warm-hearted. And last came twin boys, Lakshmana and Shatrughna, sons of Queen Sumitra.

From the very first day, Lakshmana would not be parted from Rama. If Rama was in one cradle and Lakshmana in another, Lakshmana cried until they were put together. Then he was quiet. He slept with his small hand wrapped around his brother's finger, and he never really let go, not for the rest of his life.

The four boys grew up together. They learned to ride and to shoot and to read the old books. Rama was the best archer anyone had ever seen, but he never boasted about it, and when a younger boy missed the target, Rama would say, "Good try. Watch my elbow," and show him again.

In the evenings, when lessons were done, the brothers went down to the river. Bharata and Shatrughna raced each other along the bank. Lakshmana practised with his bow until it was too dark to see the target. And Rama sat on the steps with his feet in the water and watched the boats go by, and the boatmen called out, "Good evening, little prince!" and he called back to every one of them by name.

His mother, Queen Kausalya, would come to find him there. "What are you thinking about?" she asked once. "Everything," said Rama, and she laughed, because it was true.

He noticed things. He noticed when the stable boy was sad. He noticed when the old gardener's back hurt and quietly carried the heavy pots himself. The people of Ayodhya watched him grow and said to one another, "That one will be a king to remember."

When Rama and Lakshmana were sixteen, a wandering sage named Vishwamitra came to the palace.

"King Dasharatha," he said, "there is trouble in my forest. Wild spirits come at night and knock over my lamps and scatter my prayers and frighten my students. I need a guard. Lend me your eldest son."

The king did not want to. Rama was so young. But Rama stepped forward and bowed. "I will go, Father," he said. "And Lakshmana will come too, because he will come anyway."

Lakshmana, who was already standing at the door with two bows, grinned.

So the boys went into the forest with the old sage, and they learned things there that no palace could teach. They learned to sleep on the ground and wake at the first bird. They learned which berries were sweet. And when the wild spirits came at night, crashing through the trees and howling, Rama did not hurt them. He shot arrows that burst into light and noise, so bright and so loud that the spirits ran off, stumbling and complaining, and did not come back.

"You could have driven them away for good," said Vishwamitra.

"They are only mischief-makers," said Rama. "They wanted a fright. Now they have had one."

The sage looked at the boy for a long moment and smiled into his beard.

At the end of that year, when the forest was quiet and the sage's students slept in peace, Vishwamitra said, "Come. There is one more thing I want you to see. There is a king in the city of Mithila who owns a bow, and I think you should meet them both."

And the night was quiet, and all was well.

## The Bow and the Princess

Mithila was a city of scholars and gardens, and its king, Janaka, was famous for two things.

The first was his daughter, Sita.

She had not been born in the ordinary way. One day, long before, King Janaka had been ploughing a field for a festival, and the plough had turned up a baby girl lying in the furrow, laughing, with earth in her hair. Janaka had lifted her up and known at once that she was a gift. He named her Sita, which means furrow, and raised her as his own, and she grew up as the earth's daughter should: patient, strong, and impossible to frighten for long.

The second thing was the bow.

It was a huge bow, older than the city, made long ago for the god Shiva himself. It was so heavy that it lay in a great iron box on wheels, and it took hundreds of men to move the box from room to room. Nobody had ever lifted the bow, let alone bent it to fasten the string.

"Whoever can string this bow," King Janaka had said, "shall marry my daughter."

Princes had come from every kingdom. They had strained and sweated and gone red in the face, and the bow had lain in its box and not moved, and one by one the princes had gone home.

When Rama walked into the great hall behind the old sage, nobody paid him much attention. He was tall, yes, and calm, yes, but he was a boy in forest clothes with a smaller boy behind him carrying two bows.

Sita, watching from a balcony above, noticed him at once. She noticed the way he looked at everything in the hall with interest and at nothing with greed. She noticed that when a servant dropped a tray, Rama was the one who bent to help pick it up.

Sita had spent that whole morning in her garden, the way she spent most mornings. She had a bed of jasmine that she would not let the gardeners touch, and a tame deer that ate from her hand, and a habit of talking to the trees. Her maids had had to call her three times before she came in to watch the princes try the bow, and she had come with earth still under her fingernails and a flower behind her ear, and she had leaned on the balcony rail expecting to be bored.

Vishwamitra spoke to King Janaka. King Janaka looked at Rama and raised his eyebrows and shrugged. And the iron box was wheeled into the middle of the hall, rumbling, and the lid was lifted.

Rama looked at the bow for a while. Then he bowed to it, the way you bow to something very old.

He bent and lifted it out of the box as easily as if it were a walking stick.

The hall went absolutely silent.

Rama set one end of the bow on the floor, and bent the other end down toward the string, and it bent, this bow that had never bent for anyone, and just as the string touched the notch, the whole great bow broke in the middle with a crack like a mountain splitting, and the sound rolled out of the hall and over the city and people in the streets looked up and said, "Was that thunder?"

King Janaka rose slowly from his throne.

Sita, on her balcony, laughed out loud, and then covered her mouth, and then did not bother covering it.

The wedding was the finest Mithila had ever seen. King Dasharatha came from Ayodhya with all his queens and the three other princes, and it turned out that Janaka had a second daughter and two nieces, so Bharata and Lakshmana and Shatrughna were married too, all in the same week, and there were so many lamps that the city glowed like a second sunrise.

Sita's tame deer wandered into the middle of the ceremony and had to be led out by a giggling maid. Lakshmana, who had never been to a wedding before, kept asking what happened next. Old King Dasharatha wept from the beginning to the end and said afterward that he had never been happier, which was true. And Queen Kausalya took Sita's hands in hers and looked at her for a long moment and said only, "Look after him. He forgets to eat when he is thinking," and Sita said, "I have noticed."

Rama and Sita walked around the sacred fire seven times, hand in hand, and each time round, she looked at him and he looked at her, and they both had the same feeling, which was that they had known each other a very long time and had only just now found out.

They went home to Ayodhya together in a procession that took a week to pass. The people threw flowers from every rooftop. Rama waved until his arm ached, and Sita, who was not used to cities, held his other hand tightly and looked at everything.

And the night was quiet, and all was well.

## Fourteen Years in the Forest

For twelve years Rama and Sita lived happily in Ayodhya, and King Dasharatha grew old, and at last he called Rama to him and said, "It is time for you to be king."

The whole city rejoiced. Garlands went up over every doorway. Musicians tuned their drums. Sita chose a yellow silk for the coronation, and Lakshmana polished his brother's bow until it shone.

But in one corner of the palace, somebody was unhappy.

Queen Kaikeyi, Bharata's mother, had a servant named Manthara, a bent old woman with a sharp tongue and a small heart, who had spent her whole life stirring up trouble the way other people stir soup. She went to the queen's chamber and closed the door.

"Rama will be king," she whispered, "and your Bharata will be nobody. Is that what you want?"

Kaikeyi loved Rama. Everybody loved Rama. But Manthara whispered and whispered, all afternoon and into the evening, and the whispers went into Kaikeyi's ears and got tangled up in there, until she could not tell her own thoughts from the old woman's.

"Long ago," said Manthara, "the king promised you two wishes. Anything you asked. Ask now. Ask for Bharata to be king, and for Rama to be sent away to the forest for fourteen years."

And Kaikeyi, mixed-up and frightened and not herself at all, did.

King Dasharatha wept. He begged. He said he would give her anything else. But a king's promise was a king's promise, and he had made this one long ago, and he could not take it back.

In the morning, Rama was called to his father's room. He found the old man on the floor with his face in his hands, and Kaikeyi standing very straight by the window, and he understood before anyone spoke.

He listened to the whole thing quietly. Then he knelt and touched his father's feet.

"Father," he said, "a promise must be kept. That is the first thing you taught me. Bharata will be a good king. And fourteen years is not so very long. I will go today."

Nobody had expected this. Not the king, who had expected anger. Not Kaikeyi, who had expected a fight. Rama simply stood up, bowed to them both, and went to tell Sita.

Sita listened. Then she went to her chest of clothes and began taking out her plainest ones.

"What are you doing?" said Rama.

"Packing," said Sita. "You did not think I would stay behind?"

"The forest is hard. There are no beds. No roofs. You have never..."

"Then I will learn," said Sita. "I was born in a furrow, Rama. The earth is not going to hurt me." And that was the end of that discussion.

Lakshmana did not even bother discussing. He was waiting at the gate with two bows and a bundle when they came out.

The whole of Ayodhya lined the road to watch them go. Nobody cheered this time. People wept openly, and held up their children, and some of them followed the three figures in rough forest cloth all the way to the river, until Rama turned and asked them, gently, to go home and look after his father.

Old Dasharatha did not live long after that. His heart was too full of sorrow. He went to rest among the stars with Rama's name on his lips.

Bharata had been away at his grandfather's kingdom the whole time. When he came home and heard what had been done in his name, he was so angry with his mother that he could not speak to her for a week, and so ashamed that he could not look at himself. He did not want the throne. He had never wanted it.

He went into the forest to find his brother.

He found Rama, Sita and Lakshmana living in a small hut on a hill called Chitrakoot, beside a stream, with deer grazing outside the door. Bharata threw himself down at Rama's feet.

"Come home," he begged. "The throne is yours. Father is gone. Mother is sorry. Come home."

Rama lifted him up and held him for a long time.

"I cannot," he said. "A promise was made. Fourteen years. If I break it, what will the people learn from their king? Go home, Bharata. Rule well. I will come back when the time is finished."

Bharata wept. But he understood. Before he left, he asked for one thing: Rama's wooden sandals.

He carried them back to Ayodhya. He set them on the throne. And for fourteen years he ruled the kingdom kneeling beside an empty chair with a pair of sandals on it, so that everyone would know who the real king was, and that he was only keeping the seat warm.

In the forest, the years went by. Rama and Sita and Lakshmana moved from place to place. They built huts. They made friends with sages and squirrels and river turtles. Sita learned every flower. Lakshmana learned to cook, badly, and then well. Rama learned that a hut with the two people he loved in it was more of a palace than Ayodhya had ever been.

They stayed for a while with an old sage named Atri and his wife Anasuya, who was so old that her hair was white as milk and so kind that birds landed on her shoulders without being asked. Anasuya took to Sita at once. She taught her which roots would keep through the dry season and how to make a lamp from a shell and a twist of cotton, and in the evenings the two of them sat together outside the hut while the men talked, and Anasuya told Sita stories about her own long life, and Sita listened, and afterward she said to Rama, "I want to be like that when I am old."

"You already are," said Rama. "Only younger."

And the night was quiet, and all was well.

## The Golden Deer

In the thirteenth year, they were living in a place called Panchavati, where five great trees stood in a ring beside a river, and the days were so peaceful that it seemed nothing could ever go wrong.

Far away, across the southern sea, on an island called Lanka, lived a king named Ravana.

Ravana was clever and strong and very, very proud. He had ten heads, the stories say, which perhaps means only that he could think ten thoughts at once, and most of them were about himself. He had heard of Rama, the prince in the forest who everyone loved, and he had heard of Sita, who was said to be the loveliest and kindest woman in the world.

And Ravana decided that whatever was loveliest in the world ought to belong to him.

He did not go himself, at first. He sent a friend called Maricha, who could change his shape, and Maricha turned himself into a deer.

But such a deer. Its coat was gold, spotted with silver. Its horns were like little branches of jewels. It stepped out of the trees one morning and stood in the sunlight in front of the hut, and Sita, coming out with a water pot, stopped and put her hand to her heart.

"Rama," she breathed. "Look."

Rama looked. Lakshmana looked. Lakshmana frowned.

"There is no such animal," he said. "That is a trick."

"Perhaps," said Rama. "But perhaps not. And she has had so little that is beautiful, these long years." He picked up his bow. "I will follow it and see. Stay with Sita. Do not leave her, whatever you hear."

He went after the golden deer, and the deer led him deeper and deeper into the forest, always just out of reach, until at last, far away, it turned and was Maricha again, and called out in a voice exactly like Rama's: "Lakshmana! Sita! Help me!"

The cry came faintly through the trees to the hut.

Sita went white. "That is Rama. He is hurt. Go to him!"

"It is a trick," said Lakshmana. "He told me not to leave you."

"He is your brother and he is calling for help! What kind of brother are you?"

Lakshmana had never once in his life disobeyed Rama. He had never once refused Sita. He stood torn between them for a long agonising moment. Then he drew a line in the dust across the door of the hut with the tip of his bow.

"Stay inside this line," he said. "Nothing can cross it. I will be back before the shadows move." And he ran.

Sita was alone.

The forest was very quiet. The five great trees stood in their ring with the sunlight moving through them. A hoopoe called somewhere and then stopped. Sita picked up the water pot she had put down when the golden deer appeared, and she looked at the line in the dust, and she told herself that Lakshmana would be back before the shadows moved, and she tried to believe it.

Not long after, an old holy man came slowly up the path, leaning on a staff, with a begging bowl in his hand. "Alms," he called in a thin voice. "Alms, for a hungry old man."

Sita had never sent a hungry person away in her life. She took fruit from the hut and came to the door and held it out across the line.

"Closer, daughter," said the old man. "My arms are weak."

And Sita, because she was kind, stepped over the line.

The old man straightened up. The staff fell. The thin voice became a deep one, and the rags became silk, and it was Ravana, king of Lanka, and before she could cry out he had swept her up into a flying chariot that rose from behind the trees, and they were gone into the sky, faster than any bird.

Sita did not scream. She was the earth's daughter. Instead, as the chariot flew south over the forest, she began to pull off her jewels, one by one, her bangles and her anklets and her earrings, and drop them, so that they fell glittering into the trees below like a trail of breadcrumbs.

An old vulture named Jatayu saw the chariot pass. He had been a friend of Rama's father long ago, and he flew up on his tired wings to stop it, and he could not, because he was too old and the chariot was too fast, but he flew after it as far as he could before he sank down exhausted onto a hillside.

When Rama and Lakshmana came back to the hut, it was empty. The fruit lay scattered in the doorway.

Rama did not shout. He stood very still in the doorway of the hut, and Lakshmana, who had known him all his life, had never seen his face look like that.

"I should not have left her," said Lakshmana, and his voice broke.

"You went because she asked you to," said Rama. "That is not a fault." He put his hand on his brother's shoulder. "We will find her. Whatever it takes. Come."

They found the old vulture on his hillside, and before he closed his eyes for the last time he told them: "South. Over the sea. Lanka." They found Sita's anklet in a tree. They found her earring in the grass. They walked south, following the glitter, for a very long time.

And the night was quiet, though not all was well. Not yet.

## The Monkey Kingdom

The trail of jewels ended at a mountain, and on the mountain lived monkeys.

Not ordinary monkeys. These were the monkey people, who could talk and think and build, and their king had lost his throne to his brother and was hiding in the hills with his friends. And among his friends was one who is worth a whole story of his own.

He came down the mountain path to meet the two dusty princes, a monkey with golden fur and bright kind eyes, and he bowed with his hands together.

"I am Hanuman," he said. "I am the son of the wind. You look like people who have been walking a long time. Please come and rest, and tell me who you are."

Rama looked into Hanuman's face, and Hanuman looked into Rama's, and something happened between them that nobody has ever quite been able to explain, except to say that it was the beginning of the greatest friendship there has ever been.

Rama told him everything. Hanuman listened with his whole body. And when the story was finished, he stood up and said, "Then we will find her. All of us. Every monkey on this mountain."

He brought them to Sugriva, the monkey king in exile, and Sugriva opened a bundle of cloth and showed them a handful of jewels that had fallen from the sky one day onto the mountain, and Rama held Sita's bangle in his hand and closed his eyes.

Rama helped Sugriva win back his throne, which is another story. And Sugriva, grateful, sent monkeys in every direction to search for Sita. North, east, west, they went, and came back with nothing.

Then the rains came, and nobody could travel. For four long months the sky poured down and the rivers rose and the paths turned to mud, and Rama and Lakshmana sat in a cave on the mountain and waited. Rama did not complain. But Lakshmana, who knew him, saw how he stood at the mouth of the cave every evening looking south into the rain, and how he did not sleep, and how thin he had grown.

"She is alive," Lakshmana said one night. "I feel it. She is too stubborn to be anything else."

Rama almost smiled. "She is," he said. "She would tell me off for worrying."

The ones who went south came to the edge of the world, where the land ended and the sea began, and they sat down on the sand and felt very small, until old Jambavan the bear reminded Hanuman who he was.

You may have heard what happened next. How Hanuman grew as tall as a mountain and leaped across the whole sea in one jump. How he met a golden mountain and a serpent lady and a shadow-catcher on the way. How he shrank to the size of a cat and crept through the city of Lanka, and found Sita under a tree in a garden of orange flowers, and gave her Rama's ring, and she gave him her hair jewel, and he leaped back across the sea with the good news.

When Hanuman landed on the beach and put the jewel into Rama's hand and said, "She is alive. She is waiting. She said to come soon," Rama did something he had not done in all that long journey.

He had come down out of the sky like a falling star, and the monkeys on the shore had shouted so loudly that birds rose from every tree for a mile. He had told the whole story to the whole army before Rama could get a word in, the golden mountain and the serpent lady and the garden of orange flowers, waving his arms, and the monkeys had cheered at every part. But then he had gone quiet and walked through the crowd to where Rama stood, and knelt, and opened his fist.

He wept. And then he laughed. And then he put both arms around the monkey and held him.

"You are my brother," he said. "As much as Lakshmana. As much as anyone."

Hanuman did not say anything. He did not need to.

Then came the bridge.

You cannot march an army across a sea. So the monkeys and the bears began to build a road of stone across it, hurling rocks into the waves, and Hanuman lifted boulders the size of houses, and Rama walked up and down thanking every worker by name. And a small brown squirrel carried pebbles and sand to fill the gaps, and when the monkeys laughed at her, Rama knelt and stroked her back with three fingers and said, "We could not do this without her," and that is why squirrels have three stripes.

The bridge reached Lanka in five days. The army crossed. The monkeys and the bears and the two princes stood at last on Ravana's island, with the white towers of his city shining in the distance, and the sea behind them, and Sita somewhere ahead.

And the night was quiet, and everyone slept on the sand, and tomorrow was coming.

## The Island of Lanka

Rama did not want a war.

He sent a messenger to Ravana first: Angada, the monkey prince, who walked into the great hall with its hundred pillars and said, in front of everyone, "Prince Rama asks only for his wife. Send her home, and we will go, and no one will be hurt."

Ravana laughed with all ten of his heads.

But not everyone in Lanka laughed. Ravana had a brother named Vibhishana, who was quiet and thoughtful, and who had been saying for months, "Give her back. This is wrong." Ravana had shouted at him. And that night Vibhishana walked out of the city and across the dark ground to Rama's camp, alone, and knelt, and said, "I cannot stay with my brother while he does this. Let me help you."

The monkeys wanted to send him away. Rama lifted him up.

"Anyone who comes to me asking for shelter," he said, "shall have it. That is not a rule. That is just who I am."

Then there was a great deal of noise for many days.

Inside Lanka, in the garden of orange flowers, Sita heard the drums and did not know what they meant. There was one guard in that garden who had been kind to her all along, an old woman named Trijata, and Trijata came to her under the tree and took her hands and said, "Do not be afraid, child. I dreamed last night of a prince on a white elephant, and a bright light, and a great many monkeys. It is nearly over." And Sita, who did not believe in dreams very much, found that she believed in this one, and slept.

There were drums and shouting and the thunder of feet and the whistle of arrows, and I will not tell you all of it, because it is not a story for the end of the day. It is enough to say that the monkeys were brave, and the bears were braver, and Lakshmana was hurt once and lay very still until Hanuman flew all the way to the Himalayas in a single night and brought back a whole mountain of healing herbs, and Lakshmana sat up and asked for breakfast.

And it is enough to say that at the end, on the last day, Rama and Ravana faced each other on an empty field.

Ravana was enormous, and proud, and sure of himself, and he had ten heads full of clever plans. Rama had one head, and one bow, and one thought, which was Sita.

The old sage Agastya had come to stand beside Rama, and he leaned close and whispered a few words, a prayer to the sun, and Rama closed his eyes and said them.

Then he lifted his bow, and it was quiet, and he shot one arrow.

Ravana looked at it, surprised, with all ten faces. Then, slowly, like a great tree, he lay down on the ground, and closed his eyes, and fell into a long, long sleep from which he did not wake, and the field was silent.

Rama lowered his bow. He did not cheer. He stood for a moment with his head bowed, because even a proud king who had done wrong had been somebody's brother and somebody's son, and then he turned toward the city.

Vibhishana became the new king of Lanka, and his first act was to open the gate of the garden of orange flowers.

Sita came out slowly into the light.

She had been in Lanka for nearly a year. She was thin, and her plain sari was faded, and her hair was in one long braid. But she held her head up, and she was looking for one face in the crowd, and when she found it she did not run, because she was Sita and the earth's daughter does not run, but she walked very fast.

Rama walked very fast too.

Nobody wrote down what they said to each other. Some things are not for writing down. The monkeys looked politely at the sky, and Lakshmana blew his nose, and Hanuman sat down on a rock and wiped his eyes with his tail.

And the night was quiet, and all, at last, was well.

## Home to Ayodhya

The fourteen years were nearly over.

Vibhishana gave them a gift for the journey home: a flying chariot called Pushpaka, as big as a palace and as light as a lily, that went wherever you wished as fast as you wished. Rama and Sita and Lakshmana climbed in, and Hanuman, and Sugriva, and as many monkeys and bears as could fit, and Pushpaka rose gently into the air.

They flew north over the sea, and Sita looked down at the bridge of stones and shivered and then smiled. They flew over the forest, and Rama pointed out the hut at Panchavati, and the hill at Chitrakoot, and the place where Lakshmana had first learned to cook rice without burning it. They flew over the river where the old vulture had rested.

Sita fell asleep with her head on Rama's shoulder somewhere over the mountains, and Hanuman covered her with his own shawl.

Below them the monkey mountain went by, and Hanuman leaned far out over the side and pointed down at the very rock where old Jambavan had once said, "Do you not remember who you are?" and he laughed quietly to himself. Sugriva was asleep. Half the monkeys were asleep. Lakshmana was not, because Lakshmana never slept when Rama was awake, and the two brothers sat side by side and watched the world go by beneath them without saying much, which was how they had always liked it.

Meanwhile, in Ayodhya, Bharata had been counting.

Fourteen years to the day. He had said, long ago, that if Rama did not come home on that day, he would walk into the fire. He had meant it. He stood at the edge of the city in the grey dawn in plain cloth, with the sandals in his hands, looking south.

The sky was empty.

The sun rose. Still empty. Bharata's hands began to shake.

Then, very high, a speck. Then a shape. Then a shining thing like a palace coming down out of the clouds with a monkey standing on the roof waving both arms.

Hanuman had gone ahead. He landed in front of Bharata with a thump and said, "He is coming. He is right behind me. Stop looking like that."

Bharata sat down in the dust and laughed and cried at the same time.

Pushpaka came down in the fields outside Ayodhya as softly as a leaf. Rama stepped out. Bharata ran to him and fell at his feet and Rama pulled him up and held him, and then Lakshmana was there, and Shatrughna, and the three mothers, and Kaikeyi last of all, standing a little apart with her hands twisted together.

Rama went to her.

"Mother," he said, and bowed, and she put her arms around him and wept, and whatever had been tangled in her heart for fourteen years came loose and blew away.

It was the darkest night of the year, the night of the new moon. And the people of Ayodhya, who had been waiting fourteen years, decided that darkness would not do.

They lit lamps.

Every household set a small clay lamp in its window. Then another on the doorstep. Then a row along the wall. Children ran with lamps down every street. Lamps floated on the river. Lamps burned on every rooftop and every gate and every temple step, until the whole city glowed gold from end to end, and travellers on distant roads looked up and said, "What is that light in the north?"

It was Ayodhya, welcoming Rama home.

Children who had been born while Rama was away, who had only ever heard of him in bedtime stories, stood on their fathers' shoulders to see the tall dark prince walk by and were surprised to find that he was real, and that he waved at them, and that he looked a little tired and very happy, like anybody who has come home from a long way off. Old people who had lined this same road fourteen years ago, weeping, lined it again and did not weep this time, or only a little. Someone started to sing. Everybody knew the words.

Through the streets of lamps, Rama and Sita walked to the palace with Lakshmana on one side and Hanuman on the other and Bharata running ahead, still carrying the sandals, because he had forgotten to put them down. The people threw flowers until the flowers were ankle deep. Nobody slept that night. Nobody wanted to.

In the morning Rama was crowned king, with Sita beside him as queen, and Bharata took the sandals off the throne at last and put them on Rama's feet, where they belonged, and everybody laughed.

Rama ruled for a very long time, and it was a good time. People say the rivers ran sweeter and the harvests came in fuller and nobody was hungry and nobody was afraid. They called it Rama Rajya, the reign of Rama, and even now, thousands of years later, when people want to say that something is as good as it could possibly be, that is what they call it.

Hanuman never went home to the mountain. He stayed in Ayodhya, in the palace, close to Rama, for the rest of his very long life, and when he was old he liked to sit in the garden and tell the children the story of the leap across the sea, and they never got tired of it, and neither did he.

And every year, on the darkest night, the people of Ayodhya lit their lamps again to remember. And their children did the same, and their children's children, all the way down to now.

So if you ever see a row of little lamps in a window on a dark night, you will know what they mean. They mean somebody who was far away has come home. They mean the long journey is over.

Close your eyes now. The lamps are lit. The prince is home. The monkey is asleep in the garden with his tail over his nose.

And the night is quiet, and all is well.
