Long, long ago, in a forest full of tall trees and singing birds, a baby monkey was born. His mother named him Hanuman. He had bright brown eyes, a curly tail, and legs that were much, much stronger than they looked.

Even as a tiny baby, Hanuman loved to jump. He jumped from rock to rock. He jumped from branch to branch. He jumped so high that his mother had to call, "Not so high, little one!" at least twenty times a day.

One morning Hanuman woke up very early, before anyone else. His tummy was rumbling. He looked around for something to eat, but the fruit trees were still dark.

Then he looked up.

There, just above the edge of the world, something round and golden and glowing was slowly rising into the sky. It was big. It was bright. It looked exactly like the ripest, juiciest mango that had ever grown.

"Breakfast!" said Hanuman.

He bent his little legs. He wiggled his tail. And he jumped.

Up he went, over the trees. Up, over the hills. Up, past the clouds, which were pink and soft and smelled of rain. The wind whistled in his ears, and the golden mango grew bigger and bigger.

Far below, the animals of the forest looked up and gasped. "Where is that baby going?" said the deer. "Oh dear, oh dear," said the owls.

Now, the golden mango was really the sun, of course, and the sun was very surprised. Nobody had ever tried to eat him before. He glowed a little warmer, and Hanuman felt his fur grow hot.

In the palace of the sky, the king of the gods, whose name was Indra, saw the tiny monkey sailing toward the sun. He was worried. "That baby will be burned," he said. "I must send him home."

Indra could have thrown his thunderbolt. But he looked at the small, brave, hungry face, and instead he did something kinder. He gathered a great cool wind in his hands and blew it gently, the way you blow on hot soup.

The cool wind wrapped around Hanuman like a blanket. It slowed him, and turned him, and floated him softly back down through the pink clouds.

"Oh," said Hanuman, a little dizzy. "Where did my mango go?"

Just then a pair of strong, gentle arms caught him. It was Vayu, the god of the wind, who was Hanuman's own father. Vayu held him close and laughed until his eyes were wet.

"My little one," he said, "that was not a mango. That was the sun. Nobody can eat the sun."

"It looked very tasty," said Hanuman.

All the gods came to see the baby who had jumped so high. They were not angry. They were amazed. One by one they touched his curly head and gave him gifts.

"You will always be strong," said one.

"You will always be brave," said another.

"And you will always be able to jump," said Vayu, "higher than anyone in the world. Just not quite as far as the sun."

Then Vayu carried Hanuman home through the morning air and set him down beside his mother, who had been looking everywhere.

She hugged him tight. She gave him a real mango, small and sweet and exactly the right size for a baby monkey. Hanuman ate every bit of it and licked his fingers.

Then, with his tummy full and the warm sun shining on his back, he curled up in his mother's arms. His eyes grew heavy. His tail stopped wiggling.

And far above, the great golden sun rolled slowly across the sky, keeping a very careful eye on the little monkey who had almost eaten him for breakfast.
