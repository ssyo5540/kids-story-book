---
title: Hanuman's Leap to Lanka
summary: To carry a message of hope across the wide sea, Hanuman must jump farther than anyone has ever jumped, and meet some very strange creatures on the way.
moral: Courage grows when you use it for someone you love.
reviewStatus: needs_review
---

At the very bottom of the land, where the sand ran out and the sea began, a great crowd of monkeys sat looking at the water and feeling small.

They had searched for many days for Princess Sita, who had been taken far from her home. At last a wise old vulture had told them where she was. She was on the island of Lanka, across the sea, in a palace behind high walls.

But the sea was so wide that the far shore could not be seen at all. The waves rolled in, grey and green and endless, and the monkeys sighed.

"I could jump ten leagues," said one monkey.

"I could jump twenty," said another.

"I could jump fifty," said the monkey prince, Angada, "but I could never jump back."

And then they all fell quiet, because nobody could jump a hundred leagues, and a hundred leagues was how far Lanka was.

Only Hanuman said nothing. He sat a little apart from the others, with his tail curled around his feet, watching the horizon.

Then old Jambavan, the king of the bears, came and sat down beside him. Jambavan was very old and very wise, and he remembered things that everyone else had forgotten.

"Hanuman," he said quietly, "why do you sit so still? Do you not remember who you are?"

"I am a monkey," said Hanuman.

"You are the son of the wind," said Jambavan. "When you were a baby you jumped so high that you tried to eat the sun. The gods themselves blessed you. You have simply forgotten how strong you are. Everyone forgets sometimes."

Hanuman looked at his hands. He looked at the sea. And slowly, deep inside him, something woke up, like a lamp being lit in a dark room.

He stood. He walked to the top of the great mountain that rose from the shore, and the monkeys followed and watched.

Hanuman began to grow. He grew as tall as a tree. He grew as tall as a hill. His shadow fell across the whole beach, and the monkeys had to tip their heads all the way back to see his face.

"I go for Rama," he said, "and for Sita." Then he pressed his feet into the mountain top, bent his knees, and leaped.

Whoosh! The wind of his jumping bent the trees flat. Flowers flew up from the mountain and followed him into the sky like a trail of colour. The monkeys cheered until they were hoarse.

Hanuman sailed over the sea like a golden cloud. The waves rushed by below him. Fish leaped up to look. Far ahead, there was still nothing to see but blue.

Back on the shore, the monkeys stood in a long line at the water's edge and watched until he was only a speck, and then until the speck was gone. Nobody spoke. Old Jambavan sat down slowly on the sand and folded his paws.

"Now we wait," he said. "And while we wait, we hope. Hoping is also a kind of work."

Far out over the water, Hanuman felt the hope of all those friends behind him like a warm wind at his back, and it carried him a little faster.

Now the sea had been watching, and the sea wanted to help. Under the water there lived a mountain made of gold, called Mainaka, who had been asleep for a very long time.

"Wake up," whispered the sea. "A brave friend is crossing over you. Let him rest on your shoulder."

So Mainaka rose up out of the waves, dripping and gleaming, with palm trees on his slopes and birds in his hair. "Hanuman!" he called. "Rest a while on me. Eat my fruit. Catch your breath."

Hanuman slowed. He looked at the golden mountain and he felt his heart fill up with thanks. But he shook his head.

"Thank you, kind mountain," he said. "But I have made a promise, and I cannot stop until it is kept." He reached out and touched Mainaka's peak gently with one hand, the way you touch a friend's shoulder in passing, and he flew on.

Mainaka smiled and sank slowly back into the sea, glad to have been touched by such a good heart.

The next thing Hanuman met was a great deal stranger.

Out of the water rose a huge old serpent lady named Surasa, mother of all the sea snakes. She opened her mouth wide, wide, wide.

"No one crosses my sea without passing through my mouth," she said in a voice like waves on stones. "That is the rule."

Hanuman thought quickly. "Very well," he said, "but I am rather large. You will have to open wider."

Surasa opened her mouth wider, as big as a house. Hanuman grew bigger than a house. She opened it as big as a hill. Hanuman grew bigger than a hill.

And then, quick as a blink, Hanuman shrank until he was no bigger than a thumb. He zipped into Surasa's enormous mouth, tapped her tongue politely, and zipped straight out again.

"There," he said, growing big again. "I have passed through your mouth, just as the rule says. May I go on?"

Surasa laughed so hard that the sea shook. "Clever, clever monkey," she said. "Yes, go on. I only wanted to see if you were as quick in your mind as in your legs. Go, and good luck to you."

Hanuman flew on.

One more creature waited for him, a lazy giant of the deep who liked to catch the shadows of things flying overhead and pull them down into the water. She caught Hanuman's shadow, and he felt himself being tugged toward the waves.

But Hanuman had learned his trick now. He shrank very small, so that his shadow was hardly a dot, and the giant lost hold of it and yawned and went back to sleep. Hanuman grew big again and sailed on, faster than before.

At last, as the sun turned red and low, he saw it. A dark line on the water. Then hills. Then a city of white towers and golden roofs, shining in the last of the light. Lanka.

Hanuman landed softly on a mountain top on the island and let out a long breath. He had done it. He had crossed the whole sea.

But the hardest part was still to come, because he had to find one princess in a whole city, and nobody must see him do it.

So Hanuman made himself small again, no bigger than a cat, and he waited for the dark. Then he slipped over the city wall and padded through the streets on quiet feet.

He looked in windows. He crept along rooftops. He saw great halls and busy kitchens and gardens full of sleeping peacocks. He saw the palace of the king of Lanka, whose name was Ravana, and he stayed well away from it.

But he could not find Sita anywhere, and the night was going by.

Hanuman sat down on a wall and closed his eyes. "Rama," he whispered, "help me to find her." And when he opened his eyes, he noticed a grove of trees behind a high wall, where the moonlight was very soft and the air smelled of flowers.

It was a garden of Ashoka trees, with orange blossoms and wide green leaves. Hanuman climbed the wall and dropped into it without a sound.

And there, under the biggest tree, sat a woman in a plain yellow sari. Her hair was tied in one long braid. Her face was thin and tired. But she held her head up, and she was looking at the moon.

Hanuman knew at once that it was Sita. She looked just as Rama had described her, and she had a look on her face that only very brave people have, the look of someone who has decided not to give up.

He crept along a branch until he was just above her. He did not want to frighten her. So he began, very softly, to tell a story.

"Once," he whispered into the leaves, "there was a prince named Rama, who was good and kind and loved by everyone. He had a brother named Lakshmana, who never left his side. And he had a wife named Sita, who was as brave as she was gentle."

Sita lifted her head. She looked around. There was nobody there but the trees.

"Rama searched for Sita everywhere," Hanuman went on. "He made friends with the monkeys and the bears. And one of the monkeys crossed the sea to find her, and to tell her that Rama is coming."

"Who is speaking?" Sita whispered.

Hanuman climbed down, slowly, slowly, and bowed with his hands together. "I am Hanuman," he said. "Rama sent me. He thinks of you every moment of every day."

Sita looked at the small monkey with the kind eyes, and she did not know whether to believe him. So Hanuman opened his fist, and in it lay a golden ring, with Rama's name written inside it.

Sita took the ring in both hands. She pressed it to her heart. And for the first time in many long months, she smiled.

They talked for a long while, in whispers. Hanuman told her about Rama and Lakshmana, about the monkey army on the far shore, about the bridge they meant to build. Sita listened to every word as if it were water in a desert.

Then she unfastened a jewel from her braid, a small shining ornament shaped like a flower. "Take this to Rama," she said. "Tell him I am waiting. Tell him I am well. Tell him to come soon."

Hanuman tucked the jewel carefully into his fur. "He will come," he promised. "And I will come with him."

Sita lay down under the Ashoka tree. The orange blossoms drifted over her. Holding Rama's ring, she closed her eyes, and for the first time since she had come to this island, she slept deeply and peacefully all night long.

Hanuman kept watch from the branch above her. He looked out at the stars, the same stars that were shining over Rama on the far shore, and he felt very small, and very tired, and very, very glad.

In the morning he would grow large again and leap back across the sea with his good news. But that was tomorrow.

Tonight, the grove was quiet. The princess was sleeping. And a small monkey with a great heart rested in the leaves, listening to the far-off sound of the sea.
